How to read it

What is drawn

Planar variables Xij = (pi + … + pj−1)² are the chords of the hexagon; mesh variables cij pair two sides that are not adjacent. (For adjacent sides, −2pi·pi+1 is minus a planar variable: for example −2p1·p6 = −X26.) The sliders fix six mesh variables to build the associahedron, as in the construction of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan, in the "scaffolding" chart used by Paranjape, Skowronek, Spradlin and Volovich (eq. 44): the region where every planar variable is positive. Its canonical function is the Tr(φ³) amplitude.

The δ-shift adds δ to every even–even planar variable and subtracts it from every odd–odd one. It leaves the mesh variables and the odd planar variables X14, X25, X36 unchanged, and in the chart it is the diagonal (1, 1, 1).

The pion cone is the region where every mesh variable and every odd planar variable is positive, in the space of planar variables modulo δ. With the six sliders fixed, the right view shows its slice; the other three mesh variables, c24, c26, c46, vary across it. On the cone, the pion amplitude divided by the product of the nine mesh variables is the canonical function Ω of the cone: the result this page illustrates, from a paper in preparation.

The two views

On the left, the associahedron with its shadow: the plane across δ, onto which grey lines project its vertices. On the right, the same plane seen straight down δ. A point of the right view is a whole δ-line on the left.

The shadow and the pion slice share the walls X14, X25, X36 ≥ 0, but neither contains the other. The shadow needs some shift to make every planar variable positive, i.e. Xee + Xoo ≥ 0 for every even–even and odd–odd pair; the slice needs c24, c26, c46 ≥ 0. For example X13 + X46 = c13 + c14 + c15 + c25 + c35 − X25, which the cone allows to be negative. The example "Slice outside the shadow" shows it.

Zeros

Set sliders to 0 to choose a locus. A view is outlined in green when its amplitude vanishes on the whole locus. On these sliders the pion amplitude vanishes exactly when its slice collapses (no area), and Tr(φ³) exactly when its associahedron is flat: checked in exact arithmetic for each of the 64 ways of setting sliders to 0, at several values of the others.

Among these 64 sets, four are minimal pion zeros: the "skinny rectangles" {c13, c14, c15}, {c13, c35, c36}, {c15, c25, c35}, zeros of both theories, and {c13, c15, c35}, a zero of pions only. For the last one, momentum conservation gives c24 + c26 + c46 = c13 + c15 + c35 = 0, so the slice collapses.

Why a zero happens is the balance below the views. Each mesh variable at 0 is a zero of the prefactor ∏ c. On those same walls Ω has a pole, of order at most their number, lower when the walls do not meet in a face of the cone of that codimension. When the zeros outweigh the poles, the pion amplitude vanishes.

Soft limits. The mesh variables of leg s are cas = −2pa·ps, all linear in ps, so the soft limit ps → τps, τ → 0, scales all of them by τ. Up to the δ-shift, which the pion amplitude does not feel, that is the whole soft limit: for legs 1, 3 and 5, whose three mesh variables are all sliders, it is the skinny rectangle of that leg. There ∏ c has 3 zeros and Ω a pole of order 2, so the pion amplitude vanishes to order 1, like τ: the Adler zero. The hidden zero gives the Adler zero because the pion amplitude has no pole as ps → 0 (Arkani-Hamed, Cao, Dong, Figueiredo, He, §4.1). Tr(φ³) also vanishes on these walls, but they are not its soft limit, which moves along δ and has a pole. The table under the balance follows the pion amplitude at the chosen point as τ shrinks; that point soon leaves the collapsing slice, as a generic soft approach does.

To see it: choose an example with a slice, then a leg under Soft limit (left bar), and read the table under the balance: the amplitude falls tenfold as τ does. Dragging τ to 0 shrinks the views to the leg's skinny rectangle, where the balance reads 3 zeros against a pole of order 2. To try another point, click inside the slice in the Pions view.

The outlines and the balance come from tables made when the page was built, for every way of setting sliders to 0, in exact rational arithmetic at fixed sample points; each order is read from two values of the approach to the locus, and the build stops if the samples disagree. The page also checks the zeros in floating point and says so if the two disagree.

Poles and residues

The pion amplitude has poles only on the odd walls, three-particle channels. Near X14 = 0 its residue is (X13 + X24)(X15 + X46), the product of the 4-point amplitudes of the two quadrilaterals the chord cuts off. Tr(φ³) also has poles on the six two-particle walls; the pions have none there, because a residue there would contain an odd-point amplitude, and those vanish for pions.

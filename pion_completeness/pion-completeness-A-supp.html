S1.0 Conventions on rational functions

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field of characteristic 0 and \(x\) a non-zero linear form on \(V\), \(H:=\{x=0\}\). A rational function \(f=F/D\) with \(D\) a product of linear forms not vanishing identically on \(V\) restricts to \(V\); it is regular at generic points of \(H\) if it can be written with \(x\nmid D|_V\), and then \(f|_H\) is defined; it has at most a simple pole along \(H\) if \(xf\) is regular there, with residue \((xf)|_H\). Five standard facts are used.

  • (RF1) A polynomial vanishing on \(H\) is divisible by \(x\).
  • (RF2) A polynomial divisible by pairwise non-proportional non-zero linear forms is divisible by their product.
  • (RF3) A rational function whose denominator is a product of pairwise non-proportional linear forms, and which is regular at generic points of each of their hyperplanes, is a polynomial.
  • (RF4) A polynomial that is homogeneous of degree 0 is constant.
  • (RF5) Regularity along a hyperplane and values on it pull back under a linear map whose image is not contained in the pole locus.
  • (RF6) If two linearly independent linear forms \(x,y\) are given, a polynomial vanishing on \(\{x=y=0\}\) lies in the ideal \((x,y)\) (choose coordinates in which \(x,y\) are two of the coordinates).

“\(F\equiv0\) on a linear subspace \(Z\)” always means the rational-function statement, with the relevant genericity making the restriction defined.

S1.1 The Cayley form: full proof

This subsection proves the Cayley form \(\NLSM_N=-\mathrm{Cay}_N\) used by the first-order regime of the main text (the theorem there called the Cayley form). We recall the objects. Put \(s_{ab}:=-c_{a,b}\) for non-adjacent legs and \(s_{a,a+1}:=X_{a,a+2}\), and \(Q_U:=\sum_{\{a,b\}\subseteq U}s_{ab}\) for a set \(U\) of legs (in momentum language \((\sum_Up)^2\)). A dissection \(D\) is a set of pairwise non-crossing odd chords; its faces are polygons with alternating vertex parities; \(U_f\) is the set of legs of the sides of the face \(f\) that start at an odd vertex (the leg itself for an edge side, the far block for a chord side); \(V_f:=(-1)^{|f|/2-1}Q_{U_f}\); and \[ \mathrm{Cay}_N:=\sum_D\prod_{f\in\mathrm{faces}(D)}V_f\prod_{P\in D}\Bigl(-\frac1{X_P}\Bigr), \] the sum over all dissections including \(D=\emptyset\). For a leg \(a\), the single-leg locus \(\cK_{a-1,a+1}\) is \(\{c_{a,b}=0\ \text{for all}\ b\}\), the T-separated locus of \(T=\{a-1,a+1\}\).

Lemma S1.1.1 (identities of the forms). For even \(N\ge4\), as linear forms: (i) \(X_{i,j}=Q_{[i,j-1]}\); (ii) \(\sum_{b\ne a}s_{ab}=0\) for every leg \(a\); (iii) \(Q_U=Q_{U^c}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{all}}=0\); (iv) any set of mesh variables that omits at least one same-parity pair is linearly independent; the pairs of row 1 (\(\{1,b\}\)) and of row 2 (\(\{2,b\}\)) are disjoint and together omit \(\{3,5\}\), so \(\mathrm{span}(\text{row }1)\cap\mathrm{span}(\text{row }2)=0\) for \(N\ge6\).

Proof. (i) Put \(G(a,b):=X_{a,b+1}\) with \(G(a,a)=G(a+1,a)=0\); then \(s_{ab}=G(a,b)-G(a,b-1)-G(a+1,b)+G(a+1,b-1)\) for all \(a\lt b\), adjacent pairs included, and the double sum over \(i\le a\lt b\le j-1\) telescopes to \(G(i,j-1)=X_{i,j}\). (ii)–(iii): the same telescoping over the cyclic arcs of \(N-1\) and \(N\) legs gives \(Q_{\mathrm{legs}\setminus a}=0=Q_{\mathrm{all}}\), so \(\sum_{b\ne a}s_{ab}=Q_{\mathrm{all}}-Q_{\mathrm{all}\setminus a}=0\), and \(Q_U=-\tfrac12\sum_{U\times U^c}s=Q_{U^c}\). (iv) The one relation has coefficient \(\pm1\) on every same-parity pair (the one relation \(\sum_{ee}c=\sum_{oo}c\) of the main text, which is the only relation); a set omitting such a pair contains no multiple of it. Row 1 and row 2 share no pair because \(\{1,2\}\) is adjacent. ∎

Lemma S1.1.2 (Tr\((\phi^3)\) and \(\NLSM\) vanish on single-leg loci). The Tr\((\phi^3)\) amplitude \(A_n\) vanishes on \(\cK_{n,2}\) (the single-leg locus of leg 1), hence on every single-leg locus by cyclic symmetry, and so does every coefficient of its \(\delta\)-expansion, \(\NLSM_n\) included.

Proof. With \(d_b:=X_{1,b}-X_{2,b}\) (\(d_3=X_{1,3}\), \(d_n=-X_{2,n}\)) one has \(c_{1,b}=d_b-d_{b+1}\), so \(\cK_{n,2}=\{X_{1,b}=X_{2,b}+\kappa\ (4\le b\le n-1),\ X_{1,3}=\kappa,\ X_{2,n}=-\kappa\}\) with free coordinates \(\kappa\), \(X_{2,b}\) (\(4\le b\le n-1\)), \(X_{i,j}\) (\(3\le i\lt j\le n\)). The chord forms restrict to pairwise non-proportional non-zero forms except \(X_{1,3}\mapsto\kappa\), \(X_{2,n}\mapsto-\kappa\), and \((1,3)\), \((2,n)\) cross, so every term has simple poles only. Residues: along \(\kappa=0\), \(A(1,3,\dots,n)-A(2,3,\dots,n)\), equal under \(X_{1,b}=X_{2,b}\), so 0; along \(X_{1,b}=0\), the piece \(1..b\) (at least four vertices) has row-1 mesh variables \(c_{1,x}\) (\(x=3..b-1\)), the parent's forms with \(X_{1,b}\to0\), so it lies on its own row-1 locus; along \(X_{2,b}=0\), the piece \(b..n,1,2\) likewise; along \(X_{i,j}=0\) (\(i,j\ge3\)), the piece \(j..n,1,\dots,i\) has as extra row-1 mesh variable that of its new leg, \(X_{1,i}+X_{2,j}-X_{1,j}-X_{2,i}=d_i-d_j=0\). By induction each residue is 0, so \(A_n|_{\cK_{n,2}}\) is a polynomial, homogeneous of degree \(3-n\lt0\): zero. Since \(c(v_0)=0\), \(X\in\cK_{n,2}\) implies \(X+\delta v_0\in\cK_{n,2}\), so \(A_n(X+\delta v_0)\equiv0\) there and every coefficient in \(\delta\) vanishes; the locus is non-degenerate for the odd poles by the parametrisation. ∎

Theorem S1.1.3 (Cayley form). For every even \(N\ge4\), \(\NLSM_N=-\mathrm{Cay}_N\) as rational functions.

Proof. (1) The Cayley side. (i) \(\mathrm{Cay}_N\) is homogeneous of degree 1 (\(\#\)faces \(=\#\)chords \(+1\)) and invariant under rotation and reflection: a relabelling swaps the odd-start and even-start sides of each face, and \(Q_U=Q_{U^c}\). (ii) \(\Res_{X_P}\mathrm{Cay}_N=-\mathrm{Cay}_L\mathrm{Cay}_R\): the dissections containing \(P\) correspond to pairs \((D_L,D_R)\); for a face of the left piece take the alternate class of sides not containing \(P\); its legs lie in one side \(A\) of \(P\), and the parent \(s_{ab}\) (\(a,b\in A\)) are the piece's \(s_{ab}\) with its edge \(X_P\) set to 0 (the four-term formula uses only \(X\) among the vertices of the piece, and \(a,b\in A\) are adjacent in the piece iff in the parent); the only parent mesh variable involving \(X_P\) becomes the piece's; the sign \((-1)^{|f|/2-1}\) is unchanged, and the extra \(-1\) comes from \(-1/X_P\). (iii) A leaf face (one chord side) whose far arc is \(A\) has \(V_f=\pm Q_{\text{end-parity class of }A}\), a combination of same-parity mesh variables. (iv) For every odd chord \(P\), \(X_P\equiv\sum_{\square_P}c\) modulo same-parity mesh variables (the minimal-rectangle lemma of the main text), and mixed mesh variables are independent modulo same-parity ones (Lemma S1.1.1(iv)).

(2) \(\mathrm{Cay}_N\equiv0\) on every single-leg locus. Induction on \(N\). At \(N=4\), \(\mathrm{Cay}_4=V_{[4]}=-Q_{\{1,3\}}=c_{1,3}=0\) on \(\cK_{4,2}\). Let \(N\ge6\); by (1)(i) take the row of leg 1.

Linear step. On \(\cK_{N,2}\) (parametrised as in Lemma S1.1.2) the odd chord forms restrict to \(X_{2,b}+\kappa\) (\(b\) even), \(X_{2,b}\) (\(b\) odd) and \(X_{i,j}\) (\(i,j\ge3\)): pairwise non-proportional and non-zero. By (1)(ii) each residue contains the factor \(\mathrm{Cay}\) of the piece carrying leg 1. That piece lies on its own row-1 locus by the same checks as in the proof of Lemma S1.1.2: its row-1 mesh variables are the parent's with the cut chord set to 0, and when the cut chord is \(X_{i,j}\) with \(i,j\ge3\), the extra one, of its new leg, is \(d_i-d_j=0\). So each residue is 0 by induction, and \(\mathrm{Cay}_N|_{\cK_{N,2}}\) is a polynomial of degree 1, a linear form \(\lambda\).

Pairing. Let \(P_0:=(3,N)\) and \(f_0:=(N,1,2,3)\). Then \(V_{f_0}=-Q_{\{1\}\cup[3,N-1]}=-Q_{\{N,2\}}=-s_{N2}\) (Lemma S1.1.1(iii)), and \(X_{P_0}=Q_{\{N,1,2\}}=s_{N1}+s_{12}+s_{N2}=s_{N2}\) on \(\cK_{N,2}\) (there \(s_{12}=\kappa=-s_{N1}\)); so \(V_{f_0}\cdot(-1/X_{P_0})=+1\). For \(D\ni P_0\) let \(r\) be the other face at \(P_0\) and \(\tilde r\) the face of \(D\setminus P_0\) with legs \(N,1,2\) in place of the side \(P_0\). Then \(U_{\tilde r}=U_r\cup\{1\}\), and \(U_r\subseteq[3,N-1]\) because \(r\) lies on the side of \(P_0\) away from legs \(N,1,2\). So \(Q_{U_{\tilde r}}=Q_{U_r}+\sum_{b\in U_r}s_{1b}=Q_{U_r}\) on \(\cK_{N,2}\), since every \(s_{1b}\) with \(b\in[3,N-1]\) is \(-c_{1,b}=0\) there. As \(|\tilde r|=|r|+2\), \(V_r=-V_{\tilde r}\), and \(\mathrm{term}(D)=\mathrm{term}(D\setminus P_0)\cdot V_{f_0}\cdot(-1/X_{P_0})\cdot V_r/V_{\tilde r}=-\mathrm{term}(D\setminus P_0)\). The map \(D\mapsto D\setminus P_0\) is a bijection onto the dissections without \(P_0\) and without a chord at vertex 1 or 2 (a chord at 1 or 2 crosses \(P_0\)), the empty dissection included. So \(\mathrm{Cay}_N|_{\cK_{N,2}}=\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{rem}}}\mathrm{term}(D)\), with \(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{rem}}\) the dissections \(D\not\ni P_0\) with a chord at vertex 1 or 2.

Order count. Let \(W:=\cK_{N,2}\cap\{\text{all same-parity mesh variables}=0\}\). By (1)(iv), an odd chord vanishes on \(W\) iff \(\square_P\) consists of row-1 pairs \(\{1,b\}\) (on \(W\) the same-parity and the row-1 mesh variables vanish, and the other mixed ones are independent coordinates), iff \(\odds(B_P)=\{1\}\) (every pair of \(\square_P\) must contain the odd leg 1), iff \(B_P=\{N,1,2\}\), i.e. \(P=P_0\). Take \(x_0\in W\) generic and \(y\in\cK_{N,2}\) generic. Every \(D\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{rem}}\) has at least one chord, hence at least two leaf faces, each with \(V_{\mathrm{leaf}}(x_0+zy)=z\,V_{\mathrm{leaf}}(y)\) by (1)(iii); its propagators are non-zero at \(x_0\); so \(\mathrm{term}(D)(x_0+zy)=O(z^2)\). Thus \(\lambda(x_0)+z\lambda(y)=O(z^2)\), so \(\lambda(y)=0\) for generic \(y\): \(\lambda\equiv0\). (The sign \(-1/X_P\) is what makes the pairing cancel: with \(+1/X_P\) the sum is not 0 on the single-leg locus.)

(3) Conclusion. At \(N=4\), \(\NLSM_4=-(X_{1,3}+X_{2,4})=-\mathrm{Cay}_4\). For \(N\ge6\), \(\ell:=\NLSM_N+\mathrm{Cay}_N\) has at most simple poles on odd chords (the \(\delta\)-order fact of the main text), with residues \(\NLSM_L\NLSM_R-\mathrm{Cay}_L\mathrm{Cay}_R=0\) by the pole factorisation of \(\NLSM\), (1)(ii) and induction. So \(\ell\) is a polynomial, homogeneous of degree 1, i.e. a linear form. It vanishes on the single-leg loci of legs 1 and 2 by Lemma S1.1.2 and (2), so \(\ell\in\mathrm{span}(\text{row }1)\cap\mathrm{span}(\text{row }2)=0\) (Lemma S1.1.1(iv)). ∎

S1.2 The class residue: full proofs

This subsection proves the class residue theorem (main text, thm-res). We recall its setting. \(N\ge6\) is even and \(S\in\Fpi_N\). For a set \(K\) of odd chords and \(x:=X_P|_{L_S}\) for some \(P\in K\), the hypotheses are:

  • (H1) no odd chord vanishes identically on \(L_S\);
  • (H2) for every odd chord \(Q\): \(Q\in K\) iff \(X_Q|_{L_S}\in\Q^\times x\); and \(X_Q|_{L_S}=x\) for every \(Q\in K\);
  • (H3) \(K\subseteq\cP_1\) and all members of \(K\) have the same missing flank set \(h_Q\) (so \(\square_{Q}\subseteq S\) for \(Q\in K\), and \(\ein(Q)\), \(\oin(Q)\) do not depend on \(Q\in K\));
  • (H4) \(K\) has a top \(t\) and a bottom \(b\): \(A_b\subseteq A_Q\subseteq A_t\) for all \(Q\in K\).

\(H:=L_S\cap\{x=0\}\); by (H2) it is also \(L_S\cap\{X_Q=0\}\) for every \(Q\in K\), and no odd chord outside \(K\) vanishes identically on \(H\) (a linear form on \(L_S\) vanishing on the hyperplane \(H\) lies in \(\Q x\)). \(d:=(|A_t|-|A_b|)/2\). The side amplitudes are \(a_t:=\NLSM\) of the polygon bounding \(B_t\) and closed by \(t\) (the B-child of \(t\)) and \(b_b:=\NLSM\) of the polygon bounding \(A_b\) and closed by \(b\) (the A-child of \(b\)), as functions of the parent planar variables. The claim is: \(x\cdot\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) is regular at generic points of \(H\) and \((x\cdot\NLSM_N|_{L_S})|_H=(-1)^d\,a_t|_H\,b_b|_H\).

What the proof uses of (H3) is (H3a) \(\square_{Q}\subseteq S\) for every \(Q\in K\) and (H3b) \(\ein(Q)\), \(\oin(Q)\) independent of \(Q\in K\). The statement is false for proportionality classes with no \(\cP_1\) member (a tested failure at \(N=12\)).

Sub-polygons and the grouping identity

A sub-polygon \(r\) of the \(N\)-gon is a cyclically ordered set of \(n_r\ge3\) dual points; its sides are edges or chords of the \(N\)-gon, its diagonals are chords, and its planar variables are the parent \(X\) of its diagonals. It is alternating if consecutive vertices have different parity, equivalently every side is an edge or an odd chord; then \(n_r\) is even and \(v_0\) restricts to \(\pm\) the \(v_0\) of \(r\). For a set \(K\) of odd chords, \(K_r:=K\cap\mathrm{diagonals}(r)\); \(A^{\neg K}_r:=\sum_{\mathbb T\cap K_r=\emptyset} \prod_{d\in\mathbb T}X_d^{-1}\) over triangulations of \(r\) avoiding \(K\); \(N^{\neg K}_r\) is the coefficient of \(\delta^{-(n_r-2)}\) of \(A^{\neg K}_r(X+\delta v_0)\); \(N_r:=N^{\neg\emptyset}_r\) is \(\NLSM_{n_r}\) of \(r\). A polygon's \(\NLSM\) depends only on its diagonal \(X\).

Lemma S1.2.1 (grouping). Let \(r\) be alternating and \(K\) a set of odd chords.

  1. \(A^{\neg K}_r(X+\delta v_0)=O(\delta^{-(n_r-2)})\) and \(N^{\neg K}_r=\sum_{D'\subseteq K_r\ \text{non-crossing}}(-1)^{|D'|}\prod_{P\in D'}X_P^{-1}\prod_fN_f\) (faces \(f\) of \(D'\) in \(r\)). The only denominators of \(N^{\neg K}_r\) are odd diagonals of \(r\) outside \(K\), each simple.
  2. \(N_r=\sum_{D\subseteq K_r\ \text{non-crossing}}\prod_{P\in D}X_P^{-1}\prod_fN^{\neg K}_f\). For \(K=\{C\}\): \(N_r=N^{\neg C}_r+N_LN_R/X_C\).

Proof. (a) For a triangulation \(\mathbb T\), \(\sum_{D'\subseteq\mathbb T\cap K_r}(-1)^{|D'|}= [\mathbb T\cap K_r=\emptyset]\). Exchange the sums; the triangulations of \(r\) containing \(D'\) are the tuples of triangulations of the faces of \(D'\), so \(A^{\neg K}_r=\sum_{D'}(-1)^{|D'|}\prod_{P\in D'}X_P^{-1}\prod_fA_f\). The chords of \(K\) are odd, hence not shifted. Each face is alternating, so \(A_f(X+\delta v_0)=O(\delta^{-(n_f-2)})\) by the \(\delta\)-order fact applied to \(f\) (with \(v_0\mapsto\pm v_0^{(f)}\), harmless as \(n_f\) is even), and \(\sum_f(n_f-2)=n_r-2\) because \(\sum_fn_f=n_r+2|D'|\) and there are \(|D'|+1\) faces. Taking the coefficient of \(\delta^{-(n_r-2)}\) gives the formula. Each term of \(A^{\neg K}_r\) is a product of \(X_d^{-1}\) over a triangulation avoiding \(K_r\); after the \(\delta\)-expansion the same-parity factors give polynomials, so \(N^{\neg K}_r\cdot\prod_{P\ \text{odd diagonal of}\ r,\ P\notin K}X_P\) is a polynomial. (b) Every triangulation \(\mathbb T\) of \(r\) meets \(K_r\) in a non-crossing set \(D\); grouping by \(D\), and using that the triangulations with \(\mathbb T\cap K=D\) are the tuples of \(K\)-free triangulations of the faces of \(D\), \(A_r=\sum_D\prod_{P\in D}X_P^{-1}\prod_fA^{\neg K}_f\) exactly. By (a) each \(A^{\neg K}_f\) is \(O(\delta^{-(n_f-2)})\); take the coefficient of \(\delta^{-(n_r-2)}\). ∎

The collapse identity

Lemma S1.2.2 (chains). Under (H4), two distinct members of \(K\) do not cross if and only if their odd-ended sides are strictly nested. So the non-crossing subsets of \(K\) are the chains of the order \(Q\succ Q'\iff A_Q\supsetneq A_{Q'}\).

Proof. Two distinct chords do not cross iff a side of one contains a side of the other: \(A_Q\subseteq A_{Q'}\), \(A_{Q'}\subseteq A_Q\), \(A_Q\cap A_{Q'}=\emptyset\) (that is, \(A_Q\subseteq B_{Q'}\)), or \(A_Q\cup A_{Q'}\) is all legs (\(B_Q\subseteq A_{Q'}\)). Both odd-ended sides contain \(A_b\ne\emptyset\) and lie in \(A_t\), which is not all legs, so the last two are impossible. A chord is determined by its odd-ended side, so nested means strictly nested. ∎

For \(Q\succ Q'\) in \(K\) write \(A_Q=[a,a']\), \(A_{Q'}=[c,c']\). The strips are \(\mathrm{St}(Q,Q'):=A_Q\setminus A_{Q'}=[a,c-1]\sqcup[c'+1,a']\), each of even length (\(a\equiv c\), \(a'\equiv c'\), all odd). The strip region \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) is the sub-polygon with dual points \(a,a+1,\dots,c,\;c'+1,\dots,a'+1\); its legs are the strip legs, the side \(\ell_A:=(c,c'+1)\) (the chord \(Q'\), carrying \(\sum_{A_{Q'}}p\)) and the side \(\ell_B:=(a'+1,a)\) (the chord \(Q\), carrying \(\sum_{B_Q}p\)). It is alternating. Put \(\varphi(Q,Q):=1\) and \(\varphi(Q,Q'):=N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}/x\) for \(Q\succ Q'\); \(a_Q:=N^{\neg K}\) of the B-side polygon of \(Q\) (dual points bounding \(B_Q\), closed by \(Q\)); \(b_Q:=N^{\neg K}\) of its A-side polygon; \(N^{\neg K}\) that of the whole \(N\)-gon.

Lemma S1.2.3 (collapse identity). Under (H1), (H2), (H4), on \(L_S\): \[ x\cdot\NLSM_N=x\cdot N^{\neg K}+\sum_{Q\succeq Q'\ \text{in}\ K}\varphi(Q,Q')\,a_Q\,b_{Q'}, \] and \(N^{\neg K}\), \(a_Q\), \(b_Q\) (\(Q\in K\)) are regular at generic points of \(H\).

Proof. Lemma S1.2.1(b) for the \(N\)-gon and \(K\) gives \(\NLSM_N=\sum_{D}\prod_{P\in D}X_P^{-1}\prod_fN^{\neg K}_f\) over non-crossing \(D\subseteq K\). By Lemma S1.2.2, \(D=\{Q_1\succ\dots\succ Q_s\}\); its faces are the B-side polygon of \(Q_1\), the regions \(\Omega(Q_i,Q_{i+1})\) (\(1\le i\lt s\)) and the A-side polygon of \(Q_s\). On \(L_S\) every \(X_P\), \(P\in K\), equals \(x\) (H2). Fix \(Q:=Q_1\), \(Q':=Q_s\). The \(K\)-diagonals of \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) are exactly the members strictly between \(Q\) and \(Q'\): such a member has both endpoints on the two strips, so is a diagonal of \(\Omega\); conversely a \(K\)-diagonal of \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) crosses neither \(Q\) nor \(Q'\), so by Lemma S1.2.2 its odd-ended side is comparable with both, and lying in the region it is strictly between. So Lemma S1.2.1(b) inside \(\Omega(Q,Q')\), for the set \(K\), gives \(\sum_{\text{chains }Q=c_0\succ c_1\succ\dots\succ c_s=Q'}x^{-(s-1)}\prod_iN^{\neg K}_{\Omega(c_i,c_{i+1})}=N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}\). Hence the chains \(D\) with top \(Q\) and bottom \(Q'\) sum to \(x^{-1}a_Q\cdot x^{-1}N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}\cdot b_{Q'}\), and to \(x^{-1}a_Qb_Q\) if \(Q=Q'\); \(D=\emptyset\) gives \(N^{\neg K}\). Multiply by \(x\). Regularity: by Lemma S1.2.1(a) the denominators of \(N^{\neg K}\), \(a_Q\), \(b_Q\) are odd chords \(Q''\notin K\); by (H1) \(X_{Q''}|_{L_S}\not\equiv0\) and by (H2) \(X_{Q''}|_{L_S}\notin\Q x\), so \(X_{Q''}\not\equiv0\) on the hyperplane \(H\). ∎

Reduction. If (E) \(a_Q|_H=0\) for \(Q\ne t\) and \(b_Q|_H=0\) for \(Q\ne b\), and every \(\varphi(Q,Q')\) is regular at generic points of \(H\), then \((x\NLSM_N)|_H=\varphi(t,b)|_H\,a_t|_H\,b_b|_H\): every term with \((Q,Q')\ne(t,b)\) contains a regular factor \(a_Q\) (\(Q\ne t\)) or \(b_{Q'}\) (\(Q'\ne b\)) vanishing on \(H\), times regular factors, and \(x\cdot N^{\neg K}\) vanishes on \(H\). If \(t=b\) the surviving term is \(a_tb_t\), with \(\varphi=1=(-1)^0\).

Strip regions, corners and the ear lemma

Lemma S1.2.4 (strip regions). Let \(Q\succ Q'\) be odd chords of \(\cP_0\) with \(h_Q=h_{Q'}\). On \(L_S\), in momentum language (true mesh variables \(c=-2p\cdot p\)): (i) every same-parity mesh variable of \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) vanishes; (ii) \(\ell_A,\ell_B\) sit at positions of opposite parity in \(\Omega\), and \(X_Q=X_{Q'}\); (iii) each odd strip leg is orthogonal to each leg of \(A_{Q'}\), and each even strip leg to each leg of \(B_Q\).

Proof. For chords of \(\cP_0\), \(\ein=M_e\setminus h\) and \(\oin=M_o\setminus h\), so \(h_Q=h_{Q'}\) gives \(\oin(Q)=\oin(Q')\) and \(\ein(Q)=\ein(Q')\). (1) A missing oo pair inside \(A_Q\) meeting \(\mathrm{St}(Q,Q')\) would be in \(\oin(Q)=\oin(Q')\), hence inside \(A_{Q'}\): impossible. (2) A missing ee pair inside \(B_{Q'}=\mathrm{St}(Q,Q')\sqcup B_Q\) meeting \(\mathrm{St}(Q,Q')\) would be in \(\ein(Q')=\ein(Q)\), inside \(B_Q\): impossible. (3) A pair (even leg of \(A_{Q'}\), odd strip leg) lies in \(\square_{Q'}\subseteq S\) (every even leg of an odd-ended arc is interior; the odd leg is in \(B_{Q'}\)). (4) A pair (even strip leg, odd leg of \(B_Q\)) lies in \(\square_{Q}\subseteq S\). Lemma S1.2.8 (Gram model). Let \(\mathrm{Sym}^0_n\) be the space of symmetric \(n\times n\) matrices \(G\) with zero diagonal and zero row sums, and \(X_A(G):=\sum_{a\ne b\in A}G_{ab}\). (i) \(G\mapsto(X_A(G))_{A\ \text{diagonal}}\) is a linear isomorphism onto the massless kinematic space, and the massless mesh variable of legs \(a,b\) is \(-2G_{ab}\). (ii) The image of the mesh map is exactly the kernel of \(\rho:=\sum_{\text{class }\pi+1}c-\sum_{\text{class }\pi}c\) (the one relation).

Proof. (i) \(\dim\mathrm{Sym}^0_n=n(n-3)/2\); in any Gram realisation \(X_A=(\sum_Ap)^2\), so the four-term formula gives \(c_{ab}=-2G_{ab}\). Injective: if all diagonal \(X\) vanish, all mesh variables vanish, so all non-adjacent \(G_{ab}=0\); the row sums give \(a_{l-1}+a_l=0\) for \(a_l:=G_{l,l+1}\), so \(a_l=(-1)^la\), and \(X\) of the two-leg arc \(\{l,l+1\}\) is \(2a_l=0\). (ii) Given the non-adjacent entries, the adjacent ones solve \(a_{l-1}+a_l=-S_l\) (\(S_l\) the sum of the non-adjacent entries of row \(l\)); for even \(n\) this cyclic system is solvable iff \(\sum_l(-1)^lS_l=0\), which is \(\rho=0\) (same-class entries enter with equal signs, mixed ones cancel); the solutions form a line (the \(\delta\)-line). ∎

In the Gram model \(\Lambda^I\) is: same-class entries zero except \(G_{c_\star}=G_{c_\star'}=-x/2\), and the entries of \(\square_Q\), \(Q\in I\), zero; equivalently the massive Gram model with \(G_{ss}=G_{rr}=x\), other diagonal entries 0, zero row sums, same-class off-diagonal entries 0. Write \(G_{\mathrm{mix}}\) for the mixed block (rows of class \(\pi+1\), columns of class \(\pi\)) and \(k:=n/2\). At \(x=0\), \(\Lambda_0\cong\{G_{\mathrm{mix}}\ \text{balanced}\}\) (all row and column sums 0), of dimension \((k-1)^2\), and \(\Lambda^I_0\) is the balanced \(G_{\mathrm{mix}}\) with the entries of \(\square_Q\), \(Q\in I\), equal to 0. For an odd diagonal with a side \(A\), let \(R_A\), \(C_A\) be the class-\((\pi+1)\) and class-\(\pi\) legs of \(A\); on \(\Lambda_0\), \(X_A=2\,\mathbf 1_{R_A}^{\mathsf T}G_{\mathrm{mix}}\,\mathbf 1_{C_A}=2\langle G_{\mathrm{mix}},(\mathbb P\mathbf 1_{R_A})\otimes(\mathbb P\mathbf 1_{C_A})\rangle\), \(\mathbb P\) the centring projector (\(G_{\mathrm{mix}}\) balanced).

S1.3 The children: full proof

This subsection proves the children proposition (main text, prop-child). Let \(P\) be an odd chord with dual points \(\alpha\) (odd) and \(\omega+1\), so \(A=A_P=[\alpha,\omega]\) and \(B=B_P=[\omega+1,\alpha-1]\). For \(L\in\{A,B\}\) with \(L^c\) the other side, \(k:=|L|\) and \(m:=k+1\). The child on side \(L\) has legs \(L\) in order and one new leg \(\hat P\) in place of \(L^c\), labelled so that parities are preserved: for the A-child, child dual point \(j\mapsto\alpha+j-1\) (\(j=1..m\)) and \(\hat P\) is the even child leg \(m\); for the B-child, child dual point \(j\mapsto\omega+j-1\) (\(j=2..m\)), \(1\mapsto\alpha\), and \(\hat P\) is the odd child leg 1. In both cases the map \(\delta_L\) lists the parent dual points of the sub-polygon on side \(L\) in cyclic order, with parities preserved. \(\rho_L:\cX_N\to\cX_m\) is the linear map \(\rho_L(X)_{j,l}:=X_{\delta_L(j),\delta_L(l)}\) on non-adjacent child dual points; the child pair \(\{\delta_L^{-1}(\alpha),\delta_L^{-1}(\omega+1)\}\) is a child edge, whose parent value is \(X_P\). The pair map \(\pi_L\) sends a pair \(t\subseteq L\) to itself, a pair \(\{u,w\}\) with \(u\in\intr L\), \(w\in L^c\) to \(\{u,\hat P\}\), and every other pair (inside \(L^c\), or crossing at an end leg \(l_1\), \(l_k\) of \(L\)) to nothing. It is well defined (legs of \(L\) are adjacent in the child iff in the parent, and \(l_1,l_k\) are non-adjacent in both) and onto. The child missing set is \(M_L:=\pi_L(M)\) and the child set \(S_L\) its complement: \(S_L=\{t\subseteq L: t\in S\}\cup\{\{u,\hat P\}: u\in\intr L,\ \{u,w\}\in S\ \text{for all}\ w\in L^c\}\). Its linear version is \(\pi^{\mathrm{lin}}_L:\Q^M\to\Q^{M_L}\), \((\pi^{\mathrm{lin}}_Ly)_\sigma:=\sum_{t\in M,\ \pi_L(t)=\sigma}y_t\). Write \(\mathbb Y_S:=\{y\in\Q^M: y(M_e)=y(M_o)\}\); by the one relation, \(X\mapsto(c_t(X))_{t\in M}\) maps \(L_S\) onto \(\mathbb Y_S\) with kernel \(\Q v_0\).

Statement. Theorem S2.1 (T-separated zeros; main text thm-tsplit). Let \(N\ge4\) be even and \(T\) a set of at least two legs of one parity. Then \(\NLSM_N\equiv0\) on the T-separated locus \(\cK_T=\{X: c_{a,b}=0\ \text{whenever}\ a,b\ \text{lie in different arcs of}\ T\}\).

If \(T\) has at least two legs, all of one parity, consecutive legs of \(T\) differ by an even number, so there are exactly \(|T|\) arcs, each non-empty and of odd length; legs in different arcs are never adjacent, so every condition is a mesh variable. For three legs this is the split kinematics of [CEU]; for two legs \(T=\{a,b\}\) it is the causal diamond anchored at the same-parity chord \(X_{a+1,b+1}\), and \(T=\{i-1,i+1\}\) gives the single-leg locus of leg \(i\); for a whole parity class it is \(\{c_{e,e'}=0\}\) or \(\{c_{o,o'}=0\}\).

S2.1 Quartic diagrams

For dual points \(u_1\lt u_2\lt u_3\lt u_4\) in cyclic order let \(B(u,v):=\{u,\dots,v-1\}\) (cyclically). Summing the mesh variables over two blocks telescopes (the block formula): \[ \sum_{a\in B(u_1,u_2),\ b\in B(u_3,u_4)}c_{a,b}=X_{u_1u_3}+X_{u_2u_4}-X_{u_2u_3}-X_{u_4u_1}, \] with edges read as 0; no pair in the sum is adjacent. A quadrangulation \(\Gamma\) of the \(N\)-gon on the dual points has \((N-2)/2\) quads and \((N-4)/2\) diagonals; it is dual to a planar quartic tree (quads ↔ vertices, diagonals ↔ propagators, edges ↔ external legs). A quad with corners \(u_1\lt u_2\lt u_3\lt u_4\) has four sides \((u_k,u_{k+1})\); the side \((u,v)\) carries the block \(B(u,v)\), and the four blocks of a quad partition the legs. Every sub-polygon cut off by a diagonal has an even number of vertices, so every block is odd, every diagonal is an odd chord, and consecutive corners of a quad have opposite parity.

Polarity. For \(\pi\in\{0,1\}\), a side \((u,v)\) of a quad is a + slot if \(u\equiv\pi\), otherwise a − slot. Corners alternate in parity, so every quad has exactly two + slots, and they are opposite sides. (i) The side of leg \(a\) (the edge \((a,a+1)\)) is a + slot iff \(a\equiv\pi\). (ii) Every diagonal \((u,v)\) is a + slot in exactly one of its two quads: it is the side \((u,v)\) of one and \((v,u)\) of the other, and \(u-v\) is odd. (This is the only slot labelling that the quartic rules of [CHZ, eq. (4)] allow; here the rules only define a rational function.)

Vertex factor and diagram sum. At a quad \(q\) with + blocks \(B_+\), \(B_+'\) put \[ n^\pi_q:=\sum_{a\in B_+,\ b\in B_+'}c_{a,b}=X_{u_1u_3}+X_{u_2u_4}-(\text{the two }X\text{ of the − sides of }q), \] and \(\mathrm{Q}^\pi_N:=\sum_\Gamma\prod_{q\in\Gamma}n^\pi_q\big/\prod_{C\in\mathrm{diag}(\Gamma)}X_C\). At \(N=4\) there is one quad, whose − sides are edges, so \(\mathrm Q^\pi_4=X_{1,3}+X_{2,4}=\cA_4\) for both \(\pi\).

S2.2 Every diagram vanishes on \(\cK_T\)

Fix \(T\) and take \(\pi_T:=\) the parity opposite to \(T\) (so the legs of \(T\) sit in − slots). Fix \(\Gamma\). A side of a quad is live if its block contains a leg of \(T\); a quad is good if both its − sides are live and neither + side is.

Lemma S2.2 (a good quad exists). Every quadrangulation has a good quad.

Proof (descent walk). Pick \(t_0\in T\) and let \(q_0\) be the quad with the edge of \(t_0\) as a side; that side is a − slot and live. Invariant: the current quad \(q\) has a live − side \(e\) (the entry), and some other side of \(q\) is also live. At \(q_0\) this holds because the other three blocks contain every leg except \(t_0\), and \(|T|\ge2\). Step: if no + side of \(q\) is live, the other live side is the second − side, and \(q\) is good. Otherwise pick a live + side \(D\). \(D\) is not an edge (a + edge carries a leg of parity \(\pi_T\), not in \(T\)), so it is a diagonal; let \(q'\) be the quad on its other side. In \(q'\), \(D\) is a − slot, and it is live: seen from \(q'\) its block is the union of the blocks of the other three sides of \(q\), which contains the entry block of \(q\). The other three sides of \(q'\) partition the block of \(D\) as seen from \(q\), which meets \(T\), so one of them is live. The invariant holds at \(q'\). Each step moves into the block of the exit side as seen from \(q\), and the next exit block is a proper subset of it, so the walk terminates. ∎

Lemma S2.3 (the good vertex factor vanishes). If \(q\) is good with + blocks \(B_1\), \(B_3\), every pair \((a,b)\in B_1\times B_3\) lies in \(S_T\); hence \(n^{\pi_T}_q\) vanishes on \(\cK_T\).

Proof. \(B_1\) and \(B_3\) contain no leg of \(T\), so each is a run of consecutive legs inside a single arc. The − blocks \(B_2\), \(B_4\) each contain a leg of \(T\). Going round the circle from \(B_1\) to \(B_3\) in either direction passes through \(B_2\) or \(B_4\), so \(a\in B_1\) and \(b\in B_3\) lie in different arcs; they are non-adjacent by the block formula. ∎

No boundary case needs separate treatment (\(|T|=2\), single-leg loci, \(|T|=N/2\) with single-leg blocks, arcs of length 1, blocks wrapping past leg \(N\)): the argument uses only whether a block meets \(T\).

Lemma S2.4 (no planar variable vanishes on \(\cK_T\)). For every chord \(C\), \(X_C\not\equiv0\) on \(\cK_T\).

Proof. Let \(C\) have block \(B\), \(2\le|B|\le N-2\), and complement \(B^c\). It suffices to find \(a,b\in B\), \(c,d\in B^c\) such that none of the pairs \(ab\), \(cd\), \(ac\), \(bd\) is in \(S_T\): then put \(p_a=k\), \(p_d=-k\), \(p_b=l\), \(p_c=-l\) with \(k^2=l^2=0\ne2k\cdot l\) (complex momenta) and all other momenta 0; every \(s_{xy}\) vanishes except those of \(ab,cd,ac,bd\), so the point lies in \(\cK_T\), and \(X_C=(k+l)^2\ne0\). If \(B\) and \(B^c\) both contain legs of \(T\), say \(t\in B\), \(t'\in B^c\), take \(a=t\), \(d=t'\) and any other \(b\in B\), \(c\in B^c\): each of the four pairs involves \(t\) or \(t'\), and pairs with a leg in \(T\) are never in \(S_T\). If \(B\) contains no leg of \(T\), it lies in one arc \(A\); write \(B=\{i,\dots,j-1\}\) and take \(a=i\), \(b=j-1\), \(c=i-1\), \(d=j\): \(ac\) and \(bd\) are adjacent, \(ab\) lies in \(A\), and \(cd\) either involves a leg of \(T\) or lies in \(A\). If \(B^c\) contains no leg of \(T\), exchange \(B\) and \(B^c\) (\(X_C\) is symmetric). ∎

Theorem S2.5 (every diagram vanishes). With the legs of \(T\) in − slots (\(\pi=\pi_T\)), every term \(\prod_qn^{\pi_T}_q/\prod_CX_C\) of \(\mathrm Q^{\pi_T}_N\) restricts to 0 on \(\cK_T\), and so does \(\mathrm Q^{\pi_T}_N\).

Proof. \(\cK_T\) is a linear subspace, hence irreducible. The denominator of a term is a product of \(X\)'s, none vanishing identically on \(\cK_T\) (Lemma S2.4); the numerator contains the factor of a good quad (Lemma S2.2), which vanishes on \(\cK_T\) (Lemma S2.3). ∎

With the opposite polarity most diagrams do not vanish individually (24 of 24 (\(T\), diagram) pairs at \(N=6\) are non-zero), although the sum does, by Theorem S2.1.

S2.3 The diagram sum is the NLSM amplitude

Lemma S2.6 (structure and factorisation). (i) \(\mathrm Q^\pi_N\) is homogeneous of degree 1, with simple poles only at odd chords (\((N-2)/2\) linear numerators over \((N-4)/2\) distinct odd chords). (ii) For an odd chord \(C=(i,j)\), \(\Res_C\mathrm Q^\pi_N:=(X_C\mathrm Q^\pi_N)|_{X_C=0}=\mathrm Q^{\pi_L}_L\,\mathrm Q^{\pi_R}_R\), where \(L\) is the sub-polygon with vertices \(i,\dots,j\) and \(R\) the one with vertices \(j,\dots,N,1,\dots,i\), each relabelled from 1 in the listed order with \(X_C\) as an edge, and \(\pi_L\equiv\pi-(i-1)\), \(\pi_R\equiv\pi-(j-1)\) (mod 2).

Proof of (ii). Write \(\mathrm Q=G/X_C+H\), \(G\) collecting the quadrangulations containing \(C\); no denominator of \(G\) or \(H\) contains \(X_C\), so \(\Res_C\mathrm Q=G|_{X_C=0}\). The quadrangulations containing \(C\) correspond to pairs \((\Gamma_L,\Gamma_R)\). A quad keeps its corners, so its factor is the same expression with \(X_C\) read as an edge of the sub-polygon; \(X_C\) enters \(n_q\) only when \(C\) is a − side of \(q\), and is 0 in both pictures. The + rule transports: a side \((u,v)\) of \(L\) is + iff \(u\equiv\pi\) iff \(u-i+1\equiv\pi_L\); the side \((j,i)\) is + in \(\Gamma\) iff \(j\equiv\pi\), and in \(L\) it is the edge \(j-i+1\), + iff \(j-i+1\equiv\pi_L\). The same holds for \(R\), since \(N\) is even. ∎

Lemma S2.7 (the NLSM side). Write \(A^{\phi^3}_N(X+\delta v_0)=\sum_ja_j\delta^{-j}\) at large \(\delta\). (a) \(a_j=0\) for \(j\lt N-2\). (b) \(\Res_C\cA_N=\cA_L\cA_R\) on every odd chord (with \(L,R\) as above). (c) \(\cA_N\equiv0\) on every single-leg locus \(\cK_{i-1,i+1}\).

Proof. Each \(a_j\) is homogeneous of degree \(j-(N-3)\). An odd chord is not shifted and enters only as \(1/X_C\), so \(A^{\phi^3}(X+\delta v_0)=A_LA_R/X_C+(\text{terms free of }X_C)\); on each side \(v_0\) restricts to \(\pm\) that side's own \(v_0\) (both sub-polygons are even), so \(\Res_Ca_j=\sum_{i+i'=j}a^L_ia^R_{i'}\). (a) Induction on \(N\); at \(N=4\), \(1/(X_{1,3}-\delta)+1/(X_{2,4}+\delta)\) gives \(a_1=0\), \(a_2=-X_{1,3}-X_{2,4}\). For \(N\ge6\) and \(j\lt N-2\) every term of the residue has a factor below its own leading order, so it vanishes; so \(a_j\) is a polynomial (Lemma S2.8) of degree \(j-(N-3)\), hence 0 for \(j\lt N-3\) and a constant for \(j=N-3\); that constant vanishes on the single-leg locus of leg 1 by the Tr\((\phi^3)\) row zero (Lemma S1.1.2), since that locus is cut out by mesh variables, which \(v_0\) preserves. (b) The residue formula at \(j=N-2\) with (a) on both sides; the signs agree, \((-1)^{L/2-1+R/2-1}=(-1)^{N/2-1}\) as \(L+R=N+2\). (c) \(A^{\phi^3}(X+\delta v_0)\) vanishes on each single-leg locus for every \(\delta\) (Lemma S1.1.2 and the invariance of the locus under \(v_0\)). ∎

Lemma S2.8. If \(J\) is rational with a square-free denominator dividing \(\prod_{C\ \mathrm{odd}}X_C\) and \((X_CJ)|_{X_C=0}=0\) for every odd \(C\), then \(J\) is a polynomial.

Proof. Write \(J=P/Q\); restricting \(X_CJ\) to \(X_C=0\) gives \(P|/(Q/X_C)|\) with a denominator not identically 0, so \(X_C\mid P\) for each \(C\), and \(Q\mid P\). ∎

Lemma S2.9 (three row zeros kill a linear form). Let \(N\ge6\). A linear form vanishing on the single-leg loci of legs 1, 3, 5 is 0, and so is one vanishing on those of legs 2, 4, 6.

Proof. A linear form vanishing on the common zero set of some linear forms lies in their span, so the form is \(\sum_{A_1}\alpha c=\sum_{A_3}\beta c=\sum_{A_5}\gamma c\), \(A_i\) the mesh variables \(c_{i,j}\) of row \(i\). The only relation among mesh variables (the one relation of the main text) has weight on \(c_{2,4}\), which lies in none of \(A_1,A_3,A_5\); so the variables of \(A_1\cup A_3\cup A_5\) are linearly independent, the three expansions agree coefficient by coefficient, and the form is supported on \(A_1\cap A_3\cap A_5\). The pairwise intersections are \(A_1\cap A_3=\{c_{1,3}\}\), \(A_1\cap A_5=\{c_{1,5}\}\), \(A_3\cap A_5=\{c_{3,5}\}\), and none of these variables lies in the third row, so \(A_1\cap A_3\cap A_5=\emptyset\). For rows 2, 4, 6 use \(c_{1,3}\) and \(A_2\cap A_4\cap A_6=\emptyset\). ∎

Two rows of one parity do not suffice: \(c_{1,3}\) vanishes on the single-leg loci of legs 1 and 3. Three rows are what an induction at a single polarity needs, since Theorem S2.5 makes only the rows \(i\equiv\pi\) manifest.

Theorem S2.10 (the quartic rules give the NLSM). For both \(\pi\) and every even \(N\ge4\), \(\mathrm Q^\pi_N=\cA_N\).

Proof. Induction on \(N\); \(N=4\) is in S2.1. For \(N\ge6\) let \(D:=\mathrm Q^\pi_N-\cA_N\). On every odd chord, \(\Res_CD=\mathrm Q_L\mathrm Q_R-\cA_L\cA_R=0\) by Lemmas S2.6(ii), S2.7(b) and induction at both polarities (\(\pi_L\), \(\pi_R\) take both values). Both terms have square-free denominators over odd chords (Lemma S2.6(i); for \(\cA\), the expansion in the proof of Lemma S2.7, where each triangulation contributes each unshifted odd chord at most once), so \(D\) is a polynomial (Lemma S2.8), homogeneous of degree 1: a linear form. For the three rows \(i\equiv\pi\), \(T=\{i-1,i+1\}\) has parity \(1-\pi\), so Theorem S2.5 gives \(\mathrm Q^\pi_N\equiv0\) on the single-leg locus of \(i\), and Lemma S2.7(c) gives \(\cA_N\equiv0\) there; no pole vanishes identically there (Lemma S2.4). Hence \(D\) vanishes on the single-leg loci of legs 1, 3, 5 (\(\pi=1\)) or 2, 4, 6 (\(\pi=0\)), and \(D=0\) by Lemma S2.9. ∎

Proof of Theorem S2.1. By Theorem S2.10 at \(\pi=\pi_T\), \(\cA_N=\mathrm Q^{\pi_T}_N\), which restricts to 0 on \(\cK_T\) by Theorem S2.5. ∎

S4. Verification record

Every link of the proof has a written proof for every even \(N\), and the theorem is machine-checked in Lean for every even \(N\ge6\) (§S3). The computations below were run before or alongside the proofs, as checks. None of them is a step of the proof, and none covers all \(N\). They matter in two ways: they could have refuted a false lemma at small \(N\), and several of them tested each lemma's exact hypotheses (by showing that the statement fails when a hypothesis is dropped).

Methods, in one line each.

  • Exact arithmetic: rational numbers, no rounding; an equality or a non-zero found this way is exact at the point or on the space tested.
  • Modular arithmetic: the rational functions are evaluated with all numbers reduced modulo a large prime \(p\) (usually \(2^{61}-1\)) at random points. A non-zero value proves the function is not identically zero; a zero value is a probabilistic statement, with false-positive probability at most \(k\,d/p\) for \(k\) points and degree \(d\) (and only if the numerator is not divisible by \(p\), which is why rigorous zero claims add a second random prime).
  • Independent code: the checker was written by the reviewer from the definitions, not taken from the author.
  • Exhaustive: every configuration at the stated \(N\); dihedral orbit: a class of configurations up to rotation and reflection of the \(N\)-gon.
  • SAT solver: a program that decides whether a finite system of true/false constraints has a solution; “UNSAT” means it found none.
  • LRAT certificate: a step-by-step record of an UNSAT answer that a small independent checking program verifies line by line. A “strict” checker is one that rejects the known malformed records that some checkers accept. Without a checked certificate, an UNSAT answer rests on the solver's correctness.
  • MONA: a decision procedure for a logic of finite sets of positions on a line (WS1S); it answers “valid” or “not valid” for a sentence that covers all \(N\) at once, but produces no certificate, so a “valid” rests on the correctness of one program.

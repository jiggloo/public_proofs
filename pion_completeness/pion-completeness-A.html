Abstract

Tree-level amplitudes of the non-linear sigma model (NLSM) vanish on the causal diamonds of the hidden-zero programme, and also whenever \(s_{ab}=0\) for all legs \(a,b\) lying in different arcs of the leg circle cut at a set \(T\) of at least two legs of one parity. We ask whether these are all the zeros among coordinate loci, the linear subspaces of kinematic space on which a chosen set of invariants \(s_{ab}\) of non-adjacent legs vanishes. For every even \(N\ge6\) we prove that every coordinate zero on which no planar variable vanishes identically contains one of these loci, and that each of them is itself a minimal zero; both halves use the vanishing on the loci cut at \(T\) for general \(T\), which is proved in a companion note whose proof is given in the supplement. The proof is an induction on \(N\) with two regimes: either the amplitude, to first order at a special point of the locus, equals \(\pm(\sum_{a\ \mathrm{even}}p_a)^2\), or it has a simple pole along a hyperplane on which several odd planar variables coincide, with residue the product of two lower-point amplitudes on loci of the same kind; a combinatorial lemma about three separators on a circle puts the second regime into this form. The theorem, including the vanishing on the loci cut at \(T\), is machine-checked in Lean. All proofs, written and formal, were produced and reviewed by AI model instances; no human expert has read them, and novelty has not been established.

1. Introduction

Hidden zeros. Arkani-Hamed, Cao, Dong, Figueiredo and He observed that tree-level amplitudes of Tr\((\phi^3)\), of the NLSM and of Yang–Mills theory vanish when a causal diamond of non-planar Mandelstam invariants is set to zero [ACDFH, §3–§5]. In the kinematic mesh the invariants \(c_{a,b}=-s_{ab}\) of non-adjacent legs are the natural coordinates, and a causal diamond is a rectangle of them reaching the boundary of the mesh (Figure 1(c)). For the NLSM the authors note that “all the zeros that we described for Tr\((\phi^3)\) theory are also zeros of NLSM tree-level amplitudes” [ACDFH, §4.1]. The zeros have since been derived in several other ways: from the 2-split of string and particle amplitudes [CDHS], from BCFW-type recursion [FZZ, JP], from cuts of Feynman diagrams [Zho24], from a diagrammatic representation of pion amplitudes [LWBR], and from BCJ relations [BBKOPT]. Rodina showed that every type of hidden zero is equivalent to an enhanced (“subset”) UV scaling under BCFW shifts [Rod].

More zeros of the NLSM. The NLSM has coordinate zeros that contain no causal diamond. Cachazo, Early and Umbert showed from the CHY formula that the NLSM vanishes on the split kinematics of a triple of legs of one parity [CEU, §4.1, §4.3–§4.4] (their argument does not discuss degenerate solutions of the scattering equations). The locus on which all invariants of two legs of the same parity vanish, for one of the two parities, is stated as a zero by Cao, Han and Zhu [CHZ, §IV.1]. Both are instances of one family: for a set \(T\) of at least two legs of one parity, the \(T\)-separated locus \(\cK_T\) is cut out by \(s_{ab}=0\) for every pair \(a,b\) of legs lying in different arcs of the leg circle cut at \(T\) (Figure 1(a),(b)). For \(|T|=3\) it is CEU's split kinematics; for \(|T|=2\) it is a causal diamond. That the NLSM vanishes on every \(\cK_T\) is proved in a companion note by a Feynman-rule argument (Theorem 2.9; the proof is reproduced in Supplement S2). We use the word “separated” rather than “split”, because in this literature a split is a factorisation of the amplitude near a zero [CEU, CDHS], and no factorisation is claimed on \(\cK_T\) here.

The question. If an amplitude vanishes on the coordinate locus of a set \(S\) of invariants, it vanishes on the locus of every larger set, so the coordinate zeros are described by the minimal ones. Are the known families all of them? Zhou remarks that “the hidden zeros for a given amplitude do not exhaust all zeros but instead represent a proper subset thereof” [Zho26, §2], and Feng, Zhang and Zhou ask whether there are “other zero conditions different from the one found in current literature” [FZZ, §4]. The question is open even for Tr\((\phi^3)\): that every such zero of Tr\((\phi^3)\) contains a causal diamond has, to our knowledge, only been established in part (§10).

Result in words. For the flavour-ordered tree-level NLSM amplitude with an even number \(N\ge6\) of legs, the minimal coordinate zeros on which no planar variable vanishes identically are exactly the causal diamonds and the loci \(s_{ab}=0\) across three or more cut legs of one parity. At \(N=8\) there are 30 of them (Figure 2). The precise statement is Theorem 3.1.

Why a complete list matters. Zeros have been used as input to reconstruct amplitudes. Rodina proved that Tr\((\phi^3)\) amplitudes are fixed by their zeros up to normalisation, given an ordered local propagator structure and trivial numerators [Rod]; Li and Zhou build a recursion for NLSM trees from the hidden zeros, and state that the hidden zeros together with factorisation on physical poles determine them [LZ]; and Zhou argues that locality, unitarity and hidden zeros determine the tree-level YM and NLSM amplitudes [Zho26]. A complete list tells such a bootstrap which coordinate conditions are available, and that no others exist. Zero conditions also constrain higher-derivative corrections to the NLSM, and the zeros extend via the KLT relations of the double copy to the special Galileon [BBKOPT]; whether those theories have a complete list of the same kind is open (§10).

The proof in brief. Take a set \(S\) of invariants that contains none of the listed loci; we must show the amplitude is not identically zero on the locus \(L_S\) where they vanish. The proof is an induction on \(N\) and has two regimes. In the first, we expand the amplitude at a point of \(L_S\) where every invariant of two same-parity legs also vanishes; there the only Feynman diagram of a Cayley-type expansion [KNT] that survives at first order is the single \(N\)-point contact vertex, and it gives \(\pm(\sum_{\rm even}p_a)^2\), which is non-zero on \(L_S\). In the second regime the amplitude has a pole: on \(L_S\) a family of odd planar variables \(X_{i,j}\) (with \(j-i\) odd) become one and the same linear form \(x\), and we show that \(x\,\cA_N\) restricted to \(\{x=0\}\) is the product of two lower-point amplitudes, each on a coordinate locus that again contains none of the listed loci. The residue is not an ordinary factorisation on one channel: the pole collects a nested chain of channels, and the regions between consecutive ones collapse to the constant \(\pm1\). What makes the chain nested, and the two factors again of the right kind, is a purely combinatorial lemma about three families of separated pairs on the leg circle (Theorem 8.4). The minimality half follows from the completeness half and the fact that no listed locus contains another.

Limits. The theorem is a statement about the rational function \(\cA_N\) at tree level, flavour-ordered and massless (Remark 3.2 covers a uniform mass). It concerns coordinate loci only, and only those on which no planar variable vanishes identically; zeros on other linear loci are not classified. At \(N=4\) the set and locus readings differ, so the theorem starts at \(N=6\).

2. Setup

2.1 Kinematics and the mesh

Let \(N\ge4\) be even. Legs are \(1,\dots,N\) in cyclic order, dual points are \(1,\dots,N\), and leg \(a\) lies between dual points \(a\) and \(a+1\). For non-adjacent dual points \(i,j\) the planar variable \(X_{i,j}=X_{j,i}\) is \((p_i+\dots+p_{j-1})^2\); on edges, \(X_{i,i+1}=0\) [ACDFH, §2]. The \(N(N-3)/2\) planar variables are independent coordinates on kinematic space \(\cX\), a vector space over \(\Q\) (or any field of characteristic zero); every statement below is an identity of rational functions. A chord is a pair of non-adjacent dual points. A chord \((i,j)\) is an odd chord if \(j-i\) is odd, so that its endpoints have opposite parity (the chords \(X_{e,o}\) of [AF]); otherwise it is a same-parity chord, \(X_{e,e}\) or \(X_{o,o}\). An odd chord cuts the legs into two arcs of odd length, each with at least three legs.

A pair is an unordered pair \(\{a,b\}\) of non-adjacent legs; there are \(N(N-3)/2\) pairs. Its mesh variable is \begin{equation} c_{a,b} \;=\; X_{a,b}+X_{a+1,b+1}-X_{a,b+1}-X_{a+1,b} \;=\; -s_{ab} \label{eq:mesh} \end{equation} [ACDFH, §2.1], a linear form on \(\cX\); in the mesh picture it is the tile with corners \(X_{a,b},X_{a,b+1},X_{a+1,b+1},X_{a+1,b}\) (Figure 1(b)). A pair is ee, oo or opposite-parity according to the parities of its legs. Because \(N\) is even, two legs of the same parity are never adjacent, so every same-parity pair of legs is a pair. Let \(v_0\in\cX\) be the vector with \(X_{e,e}(v_0)=+1\) on chords with two even endpoints, \(X_{o,o}(v_0)=-1\) on chords with two odd endpoints, and \(X(v_0)=0\) on odd chords.

Lemma 2.1 (the one relation: momentum conservation). As linear forms on \(\cX\), \(\sum_{ee}c_{a,b}=\sum_{oo}c_{a,b}\), where the sums run over all ee pairs and all oo pairs. The kernel of the map \(X\mapsto(c_t(X))_t\) is \(\Q v_0\), and every linear relation among the mesh variables is a multiple of this one. Write \(E:=\sum_{ee}c=\sum_{oo}c\), the parity form; in momentum language \(E=-\bigl(\sum_{a\ \mathrm{even}}p_a\bigr)^2=-\bigl(\sum_{a\ \mathrm{odd}}p_a\bigr)^2\).

The proof is Lemma 5.1.

2.2 The amplitude

The tree-level Tr\((\phi^3)\) amplitude is \(A^{\phi^3}_N(X)=\sum_{\TT}\prod_{d\in\TT}1/X_d\), the sum over the triangulations \(\TT\) of the \(N\)-gon on the dual points. The \(\delta\)-shift replaces \(X_{e,e}\to X_{e,e}+\delta\), \(X_{o,o}\to X_{o,o}-\delta\) and keeps the odd chords, that is \(X\mapsto X+\delta v_0\); it preserves every mesh variable, and at large \(\delta\) the leading term of the shifted amplitude is the NLSM amplitude [ACDFH, §7.1, §7.4.1; ACDFH24, eq. (2)].

Definition 2.2 (normalisation). \(\NLSM_N\) is the coefficient of \(\delta^{-(N-2)}\) in the large-\(\delta\) expansion of \(A^{\phi^3}_N(X+\delta v_0)\), and \(\cA_N:=(-1)^{N/2-1}\NLSM_N\). Thus \(\NLSM_4=-(X_{1,3}+X_{2,4})\) and \(\cA_4=X_{1,3}+X_{2,4}\).

The two differ by a sign, which affects no zero. Proofs are written for \(\NLSM_N\); the one place where the sign matters (the residue, Theorem 7.9) states both forms. Reading \(\cA_N\) as the Lagrangian pion amplitude uses the identification of [AF, §2–§3], which the proofs do not use.

Facts 2.3 (analytic inputs). Let \(N\ge4\) be even.

  1. (\(\delta\)-order.) In \(A^{\phi^3}_N(X+\delta v_0)=\sum_j a_j(X)\delta^{-j}\) every \(a_j\) with \(j\lt N-2\) vanishes. \(\NLSM_N=a_{N-2}\) is homogeneous of degree 1, and \(\NLSM_N\cdot\prod_{P\ \mathrm{odd}}X_P\) is a polynomial: the only poles are simple poles on odd chords.
  2. (Factorisation.) For an odd chord \(C\), \(\Res_{X_C=0}\NLSM_N:=(X_C\NLSM_N)|_{X_C=0}=\NLSM_L\cdot\NLSM_R\), where \(L,R\) are the two sub-polygons cut off by \(C\), each relabelled, with \(X_C\) read as an edge; the sign is \(+1\) for the raw coefficient.
  3. (Symmetry.) \(\NLSM_N\) is invariant under the dihedral relabellings of the legs and under \(v_0\mapsto-v_0\).

Where these come from. A triangulation is the same as a set \(D\) of pairwise non-crossing odd chords together with a triangulation of each face of \(D\) by same-parity chords; in an alternating \(2m\)-gon such a triangulation has all its ears on one parity class, so the face contributes \(\sum_{\pm}\bigl[\pm\delta^{-(2m-3)}\mathrm{Cat}(m-2)-\delta^{-(2m-2)}\sum_{\TT\in\TT_\pm}\sum_{Q\in\TT}X_Q\bigr]+O(\delta^{-(2m-1)})\), where \(\TT_\pm\) are the triangulations of the face by chords of the class \(\pm\); the first terms cancel between the two classes, and \(\sum_f(|f|-2)=N-2\). This gives (a) and the Catalan form \(\NLSM_N=\sum_D\prod_{P\in D}X_P^{-1}\prod_fW_f\) of [AF, §2]. (b) follows because an odd chord is unshifted and \(v_0\) restricts to \(\pm\) the \(v_0\) of each alternating sub-polygon. A grouping identity that refines (b) is Lemma 7.7.

2.3 Coordinate loci and zeros

Definition 2.4 (coordinate loci). For a set \(S\) of pairs, the coordinate locus is the linear subspace \(L_S:=\{X\in\cX: c_t(X)=0\ \text{for all}\ t\in S\}\). The set \(S\) is non-degenerate if no planar variable vanishes identically on \(L_S\). \(S\) is a zero if \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\equiv0\). A non-degenerate coordinate zero is a non-degenerate \(S\) that is a zero.

Since every mesh variable vanishes on \(v_0\), \(v_0\in L_S\) for every \(S\), and a same-parity chord never vanishes identically on \(L_S\): non-degeneracy is a condition on odd chords only. For non-degenerate \(S\) the denominator \(\prod_{P\ \mathrm{odd}}X_P\) of \(\NLSM_N\) does not vanish identically on \(L_S\), so \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) is a well-defined rational function, and “\(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\)” means that \(\NLSM_N(x)\ne0\) at some \(x\in L_S\) where no odd chord vanishes. On a degenerate locus some propagator vanishes identically, the restriction is not defined, and the zero condition is vacuous; this is why the theorem is stated for non-degenerate sets. Subsets of a non-degenerate set are non-degenerate.

Definition 2.5 (minimal zero). A non-degenerate coordinate zero \(Z\) is minimal if \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_{Z\setminus\{t\}}\) for every pair \(t\in Z\).

2.4 The list \(\cG_N\)

Definition 2.6 (separated pairs). For a set \(G\) of legs, the arcs of \(G\) are the maximal runs of legs not in \(G\), and \(S_G\) is the set of pairs \(\{u,v\}\), \(u,v\notin G\), whose legs lie in different arcs of \(G\). (Such legs are never adjacent.)

Definition 2.7 (the list).

  • \(T\)-separated sets: \(S_T\) for a set \(T\) of at least two legs, all of one parity. Its locus \(\cK_T:=L_{S_T}\), the \(T\)-separated locus, is cut out by \(s_{ab}=0\) whenever \(a,b\) lie in different arcs of \(T\).
  • Odd-chord diamonds: \(S_{\{a,b\}}\) for two legs \(a,b\) of different parity whose two arcs are both non-empty. It is the causal diamond of [ACDFH, §3.2] with corner at the odd chord \(X_{a+1,b+1}\).
  • \(\cG_N:=\{S_T: |T|\ge2,\ T\ \text{of one parity}\}\cup\{\text{odd-chord diamonds}\}\).

Equivalently, \(\cG_N\) consists of all causal diamonds together with the \(S_T\) with \(|T|\ge3\): a causal diamond with corner at a same-parity chord is an \(S_T\) with \(|T|=2\). A special case used below is the single-leg locus \(\cK_{\{a-1,a+1\}}=\{c_{a,b}=0\ \text{for all}\ b\}\), a skinny causal diamond.

The causal diamonds are zeros of Tr\((\phi^3)\) and of the NLSM [ACDFH, §3–§4]; derivations are cited in §1. For the Tr\((\phi^3)\) diamond zero there is also a machine-checked proof in the formalisation library of this project, which the Lean proof of Theorem 3.1 uses (§Methods). The general \(T\)-separated zeros rest on the companion note:

Theorem 2.9 (\(T\)-separated zeros). For every even \(N\ge4\) and every set \(T\) of at least two legs of one parity, \(\NLSM_N\equiv0\) on \(\cK_T\).

For \(|T|=3\) this is [CEU, §4.1, §4.3–§4.4]; for \(T\) a whole parity class it is stated in [CHZ, §IV.1]. The companion note proves the general case diagram by diagram, with the quartic Feynman rules of [CHZ]; its proof uses Facts 2.3(a)–(b), the one relation and the Tr\((\phi^3)\) zero on single-leg loci, and is given in Supplement S2. Theorem 2.9 is used in both clauses of the main theorem: in clause 2 directly, and in clause 1 through Lemma 7.14 and Theorem 7.17, where it is applied to sub-polygons.

3. The theorem and examples

Theorem 3.1. Let \(N\ge6\) be even. For the flavour-ordered tree-level NLSM amplitude \(\cA_N\):

  1. (completeness) every non-degenerate coordinate zero contains a member of \(\cG_N\), that is, some \(S_T\) with \(|T|\ge2\) legs of one parity or an odd-chord diamond;
  2. (minimality) every member of \(\cG_N\) is a minimal non-degenerate coordinate zero.

Hence the minimal non-degenerate coordinate zeros of \(\cA_N\) are exactly the members of \(\cG_N\): the causal diamonds and the loci \(s_{ab}=0\) for all pairs of legs separated by three or more cut legs of one parity. The proof uses the \(T\)-separated zero theorem (Theorem 2.9, proved in the companion note; Supplement S2) in both clauses.

Proof of “hence”. Let \(Z\) be a minimal zero. Clause 1 gives a member \(S_G\in\cG_N\) with \(S_G\subseteq Z\). If \(S_G\ne Z\), pick a pair \(t\in Z\setminus S_G\); then \(Z\setminus\{t\}\supseteq S_G\), so \(L_{Z\setminus\{t\}}\subseteq L_{S_G}\), and \(\NLSM_N\) vanishes there by clause 2, contradicting minimality. So \(Z=S_G\). ∎

Clause 1 is proved in §4–§8, clause 2 in §9.

Remarks 3.2 (scope).

  1. Tree level, flavour-ordered, massless. A massive version is not proved here. For the \(U(N_f)\) NLSM with the uniform spurion mass term of [CGW, §V.2], that paper derives the relation \(\mathrm{mNLSM}_N(X)=\NLSM_N(X-m^2)\) with every \(c\) unchanged; granted this relation, the theorem transfers, with the same coordinate loci \(\{c=0\}\) and non-degeneracy read for the shifted propagators \(X-m^2\). Which flavour groups the relation covers (\(U(N_f)\), \(SU(2)\), \(SU(N_f\ge3)\)) is discussed in the companion note in a part not reproduced in the supplement, and we do not rely on it; a naive uniform mass term does not preserve the hidden zeros [CGW, §V.1]. Such massive loci contain no real momenta (\(|p\cdot q|\ge m^2\) for real timelike \(p,q\)), so a massive statement would be an identity of rational functions at complex kinematics.
  2. \(N\ge6\). At \(N=4\) the set and locus readings differ: \(\{c_{1,3}\}\) and \(\{c_{2,4}\}\) are two minimal zero sets with one locus (the one relation reads \(c_{2,4}=c_{1,3}\) there).
  3. Sets and loci. Every member of \(\cG_N\) is linearly closed (the span of its mesh variables contains no other mesh variable; Proposition 9.1(c)), so “minimal” means the same over sets of pairs and over loci.
  4. Which function. The theorem is about the rational function \(\cA_N\) of Definition 2.2.

Example 3.3 (\(N=6\)). There are 9 pairs, and \(\cG_6\) has 11 members: the six \(S_T\) with \(|T|=2\), for instance \(S_{\{1,3\}}=\{\{2,4\},\{2,5\},\{2,6\}\}\) (the single-leg locus \(c_{2,b}=0\)); the two \(S_T\) with \(|T|=3\), for instance \(S_{\{1,3,5\}}=\{\{2,4\},\{2,6\},\{4,6\}\}\), the set of all ee pairs; and the three odd-chord diamonds \(S_{\{a,a+3\}}\), for instance \(S_{\{1,4\}}=\{\{2,5\},\{2,6\},\{3,5\},\{3,6\}\}\), with corner at \(X_{2,5}\). The set \(\{\{2,4\},\{2,6\},\{4,6\}\}\) is the first example (smallest \(N\)) of a zero that contains no causal diamond: \(s_{24}=s_{26}=s_{46}=0\), i.e. the three even momenta are mutually orthogonal, and \(\cA_6\) vanishes.

Example 3.4 (\(N=8\)). There are 20 pairs, and \(\cG_8\) has 30 members (Figure 2): eight \(S_{\{a,a+2\}}\) (5 pairs each), four \(S_{\{a,a+4\}}\) (9 pairs), eight \(S_T\) with \(|T|=3\) (7 pairs), two with \(|T|=4\) (6 pairs) and eight odd-chord diamonds \(S_{\{a,a+3\}}\) (8 pairs). Theorem 3.1 says that each of these 30 sets is a zero, that removing any one of its pairs gives a set on which \(\cA_8\not\equiv0\) (208 such drop-one sets in all), and that every non-degenerate coordinate zero contains one of them.

4. Proof strategy

4.1 The argument on one page

Fix \(S\) and write \(M\) for the missing set, the pairs not in \(S\). Clause 1 says: if \(M\) meets every member of \(\cG_N\), then \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\). We call such \(S\) candidate sets and write \(\Fc_N\) for them. The proof is a strong induction on \(N\) (Figure 3).

  1. Linear algebra (§5). On \(L_S\) the missing mesh variables \(y_t=c_t|_{L_S}\) are coordinates subject to one relation, and every odd planar variable is an explicit sum of them (the chord formula, Theorem 4.3). This decides which odd chords vanish or coincide on \(L_S\), and it shows that candidate sets are automatically non-degenerate.
  2. Two regimes. A family \(\cP_1\) of odd chords, read off from \(M\) (Definition 4.5), decides the regime. If \(\cP_1=\emptyset\) (§6), expand \(\NLSM_N\) at a point of \(L_S\) where also every same-parity invariant vanishes: at first order only the \(N\)-point contact vertex of a Cayley-type Feynman expansion survives, and it equals \(\pm E\ne0\). If \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\) (§7), several odd planar variables restrict to one linear form \(x\) on \(L_S\), and \(\NLSM_N\) has a simple pole along \(x=0\).
  3. Residue = product of lower-point amplitudes (§7). The chords with restriction \(x\) form a nested chain from a top \(t\) to a bottom \(b\); the residue is \(\pm\NLSM\) of the polygon beyond \(t\) times \(\pm\NLSM\) of the polygon inside \(b\), each restricted to a coordinate locus of the same kind. By induction both factors are non-zero, so the residue, and hence \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\), is non-zero.
  4. Combinatorial trapping (§8). Two things must be shown about \(M\): the chain has a bottom whose inner side carries no “free cut”, and the two children are again candidate sets. Both reduce to one statement about three families of separated pairs on a circle, (Tri), whose proof uses no amplitude.
  5. Minimality (§9). Removing one pair from a member of \(\cG_N\) gives a candidate set, because \(\cG_N\) is an antichain; clause 1 then gives non-vanishing.

4.2 The objects

Definition 4.1 (missing set). For a set \(S\) of pairs, \(M\) is the set of pairs not in \(S\); \(M_e\), \(M_o\), \(\Mix\) are the missing pairs that are ee, oo and opposite-parity, and \(\Msame=M_e\cup M_o\). For \(t\in M\) put \(y_t:=c_t|_{L_S}\), and \(y(h):=\sum_{t\in h}y_t\) for \(h\subseteq M\).

Definition 4.2 (sides, flank sets and rectangle of an odd chord). An odd chord \(P\) divides the legs into two arcs of odd size, each with at least three legs: the odd-ended side \(A_P\), whose two end legs are odd, and the even-ended side \(B_P\), whose end legs are even (Figure 4). A chord is determined by its odd-ended side. For an arc \(L\), \(\evens(L)\) and \(\odds(L)\) are its even and odd legs. The chord carries two members of \(\cG_N\), its flank sets \(\FlA_P:=S_{\odds(A_P)}\) and \(\FlB_P:=S_{\evens(B_P)}\); its missing flank set is \(h_P:=M\cap(\FlA_P\cup\FlB_P)\). Its rectangle is \(\rect{P}:=\evens(A_P)\times\odds(B_P)\), the opposite-parity pairs joining an even leg of the odd-ended side to an odd leg of the even-ended side; \(\rect{P}=\FlA_P\cap\FlB_P\) (Proposition 5.4), and \(X_P\) equals \(\sum_{t\in\rect{P}}c_t\) modulo same-parity mesh variables (Lemma 6.2).

Theorem 4.3 (chord formula). For every set \(S\) of pairs and every odd chord \(P\), \begin{equation} X_P|_{L_S} \;=\; y(h_P)-E|_{L_S}. \label{eq:chord} \end{equation} The right-hand side is a function on \(L_S\); as a vector of coefficients in the \(y_t\) it is determined only modulo the one relation \(y(M_e)=y(M_o)\), which is why \(E\) appears.

So on a coordinate locus an odd planar variable is fixed by which invariants of its two flank sets are missing; in particular two odd chords with the same missing flank set coincide on \(L_S\). The proof is in §5.

Proposition 4.4 (transversal form). Let \(N\ge4\) be even. A set \(S\) is a candidate set, \(S\in\Fc_N\) (non-degenerate and containing no member of \(\cG_N\)), if and only if \(M\) meets every member of \(\cG_N\), that is, \(M\) is a transversal (hitting set) of the family \(\cG_N\) viewed as a hypergraph on the pairs. Non-degeneracy is then automatic, and \(\Fc_N\) is closed under taking subsets.

The proof is Proposition 5.6. From now on “\(S\in\Fc_N\)” is used only through this form: for a named member \(S_G\) of \(\cG_N\), \(M\cap S_G\ne\emptyset\). Clause 1 of Theorem 3.1 says exactly that \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\) for every \(S\in\Fc_N\).

Definition 4.5 (chord families). For an odd chord \(P\): \(\ein(P)\) is the set of missing ee pairs inside \(B_P\) and \(\oin(P)\) the set of missing oo pairs inside \(A_P\) (a pair is inside an arc if both legs are in it).

  • \(\cP_0:=\{P\ \text{odd}: \rect{P}\cap M=\emptyset\}\) (equivalently, the two flank sets of \(P\) share no missing pair).
  • \(\cP_1:=\{P\in\cP_0: \ein(P)\ne\emptyset\ \text{and}\ \oin(P)\ne\emptyset\}\).
  • The base locus is \(L_S^0:=L_{S\cup\mathrm{Same}}\subseteq L_S\), where \(\mathrm{Same}\) is the set of all same-parity pairs; on it every same-parity invariant vanishes as well.

The meaning: \(X_P\) vanishes on the base locus exactly when \(P\in\cP_0\) (Lemma 6.2), so the chords of \(\cP_0\) are the propagators that go on shell at the expansion point of the first regime. Those in \(\cP_0\setminus\cP_1\) contribute nothing at first order (Lemma 6.7); \(\cP_1\) is exactly where the first-order argument can fail, and there the residue argument takes over. By the flank-set dictionary (Proposition 5.4), \(\ein(P)=M_e\setminus h_P\) and \(\oin(P)=M_o\setminus h_P\).

4.3 A worked example of both regimes

Example 4.6 (\(N=8\)). Both sets below are maximal candidate sets: their missing sets are minimal transversals of \(\cG_8\) (Figure 5).

(a) First-order regime. \(M=\{\{1,3\},\{2,5\},\{4,7\},\{6,8\}\}\), so \(M_o=\{\{1,3\}\}\), \(M_e=\{\{6,8\}\}\) and \(\Mix=\{\{2,5\},\{4,7\}\}\). The chords whose rectangle avoids \(M\) are \(\cP_0=\{X_{2,5},X_{2,7},X_{3,8},X_{5,8}\}\); each has missing flank set \(\{\{1,3\},\{6,8\}\}\), so by the chord formula each equals \(y_{1,3}+y_{6,8}-E=E\) on \(L_S\) (the one relation gives \(y_{1,3}=y_{6,8}=E\) here), and each has \(\ein=\oin=\emptyset\). So \(\cP_1=\emptyset\). Theorem 6.8 gives, for a generic point \(x_0\) of the base locus and a generic \(y\in L_S\), \(\cA_8(x_0+z\,y)=z\,E(y)+O(z^2)\); since \(E\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\), \(\cA_8\not\equiv0\) there.

(b) Residue regime. \(M=\{\{1,3\},\{1,4\},\{2,6\},\{5,7\},\{6,8\}\}\), so \(M_o=\{\{1,3\},\{5,7\}\}\), \(M_e=\{\{2,6\},\{6,8\}\}\), \(\Mix=\{\{1,4\}\}\). Now \(\cP_1=\{X_{1,4},X_{1,6}\}\), with odd-ended sides \(\{1,2,3\}\subset\{1,\dots,5\}\); both have missing flank set \(\{\{2,6\},\{5,7\}\}\), \(\ein=\{\{6,8\}\}\) and \(\oin=\{\{1,3\}\}\), so both restrict to the same form \(x:=X_{1,6}|_{L_S}=X_{1,4}|_{L_S}\). They form one flank class with top \(t=X_{1,6}\) and bottom \(b=X_{1,4}\); the strip between them is the legs \(\{4,5\}\), so \(d=1\). The residue theorem (Theorem 7.9) gives, on \(H=L_S\cap\{x=0\}\), \[ \bigl(x\cdot\cA_8|_{L_S}\bigr)\big|_H=\cA_4(\text{legs }6,7,8,\hat t)\cdot\cA_4(\text{legs }1,2,3,\hat b) =(X_{6,8}+X_{1,7})\,(X_{1,3}+X_{2,4})\big|_H, \] where \(\hat t,\hat b\) are the new massless legs replacing the far sides of \(t\) and \(b\). Both children have four legs and empty child sets, which are candidate sets at \(N=4\), and the product is non-zero on \(H\); so \(\cA_8\) has a genuine pole along \(x=0\) and \(\cA_8\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\). The region between \(t\) and \(b\) (legs 4, 5 and the two chords) is a four-point amplitude that on \(H\) contributes the factor \((-1)^d\) of Theorem 7.9 in the \(\NLSM\) normalisation, which the sign convention of \(\cA\) absorbs.

The amplitude statements of Example 4.6 follow from the theorems cited; as an illustration they were also evaluated on random lines (Supplement S4).

4.4 The induction

Clause 1 is proved by strong induction on \(N\), with base \(N=4\): \(\Fc_4=\{\emptyset\}\) (each of the two pairs \(\{1,3\}\), \(\{2,4\}\) is by itself a member of \(\cG_4\)) and \(\NLSM_4=-(X_{1,3}+X_{2,4})\not\equiv0\). Let \(N\ge6\) and \(S\in\Fc_N\).

  1. If \(\cP_1=\emptyset\): the first-order term of \(\NLSM_N\) at a generic point of \(L_S^0\) along \(L_S\) is \(\varepsilon_NE\) with \(\varepsilon_N=\pm1\), and \(E\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\) (Theorem 6.8).
  2. If \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\): an innermost chord \(P^*\) exists (Corollary 7.3): a chord of \(\cP_1\) whose even-ended side is linked (no free cut, Definition 7.1) and whose odd-ended side is minimal among such chords. The combinatorial core gives, through Lemma 8.9 and Proposition 8.15, a chord \(b\) in the flank class \(K^*\) of \(P^*\) (the chords of \(\cP_1\) with the same missing flank set) whose odd-ended side is linked; so \(K^*\) has top \(P^*\) and bottom \(b\) (Proposition 7.5).
  3. Proportionality and residue: \(K^*\) is the full class of odd chords proportional to \(X_{P^*}\) on \(L_S\), every member at \(+X_{P^*}\) (Lemma 7.6); hence \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) has at most a simple pole along \(X_{P^*}=0\), with residue \(\pm\NLSM(B\text{-child of }P^*)\cdot\NLSM(A\text{-child of }b)\) (Theorem 7.9). The B-child of a chord is the polygon on its even-ended side closed by one new leg, the A-child the polygon on its odd-ended side.
  4. Children: both children are coordinate loci of candidate sets at even sizes between 4 and \(N-2\), and the restriction maps onto them are surjective (Proposition 7.18); by induction the residue is a product of two non-zero rational functions on a linear space, hence non-zero.

The first regime cannot be replaced by a residue: on some \(S\in\Fc_N\) with \(\cP_1=\emptyset\), \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) has no pole and equals the linear form \(\varepsilon_NE\) (Remark 6.9). No step uses maximality of \(S\).

Well-foundedness. Clause 1 at size \(N\) calls clause 1 only at the two child sizes, each at most \(N-2\) (an odd chord has at least three legs on each side). Clause 1 never calls clause 2, and clause 2 at size \(N\) calls clause 1 at size \(N\). The combinatorial definitions call no amplitude. The analytic inputs are Facts 2.3, the one relation, the Tr\((\phi^3)\) diamond (rectangle) zero, used in Theorem 6.4 and Proposition 9.1(b), and Theorem 2.9, used in the residue step and in clause 2.

5. Linear algebra of the mesh

Everything in this section is linear algebra over \(\Q\); nothing depends on the amplitude. The physical content is momentum conservation: the mesh variables are the \(-s_{ab}\) of non-adjacent legs, and their only linear relation is \((\sum_{\rm even}p)^2=(\sum_{\rm odd}p)^2\).

Lemma 5.1 (the one relation; Lemma 2.1 in full). Let \(N\ge4\) be even, and let \(\mathbf c:\cX\to\Q^{\mathrm{pairs}}\), \(X\mapsto(c_t(X))_t\).

  1. \(\sum_{ee}c_t=\sum_{oo}c_t\) as linear forms.
  2. \(\ker\mathbf c=\Q v_0\).
  3. \(\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf c=N(N-3)/2-1\), and every relation \(\sum_t\lambda_tc_t=0\) has \(\lambda=\kappa\varrho\), with \(\varrho_t=+1\) on ee pairs, \(-1\) on oo pairs and \(0\) on opposite-parity pairs.
  4. \(\mathrm{im}\,\mathbf c=\{y: \sum_{ee}y=\sum_{oo}y\}\).

Proof. (i) Fix a leg \(i\) and sum \(c_{i,j}\) over the legs \(j\) non-adjacent to \(i\) (\(j=i+2,\dots,i-2\)). The two telescoping sums give \(\sum_j(X_{i,j}-X_{i,j+1})=X_{i,i+2}\) (as \(X_{i,i-1}\) is an edge) and \(\sum_j(X_{i+1,j+1}-X_{i+1,j})=X_{i-1,i+1}\). So the row sum is \(r_i=y_i+y_{i-1}\) with \(y_m:=X_{m,m+2}\), and \(\sum_i(-1)^ir_i=0\) because \((-1)^i\) is well defined modulo the even number \(N\). On the other hand \(\sum_i(-1)^ir_i=\sum_{\{a,b\}}((-1)^a+(-1)^b)c_{a,b}=2\sum_{ee}c-2\sum_{oo}c\).

(ii) If \(b-a\) is even, \(X_{a,b}\) and \(X_{a+1,b+1}\) are same-parity chords of opposite classes and contribute \(+1\) and \(-1\) to \(c_{a,b}(v_0)\), while \(X_{a,b+1},X_{a+1,b}\) are odd chords or edges; if \(b-a\) is odd the roles swap. So \(v_0\in\ker\mathbf c\). Conversely let \(\mathbf c(X)=0\) and \(y_m:=X_{m,m+2}\) for \(m=1,\dots,N-2\). Claim A: \(X_{i,j}=\sum_{m=i}^{j-2}y_m\) for every chord \(1\le i\lt j\le N\), \((i,j)\ne(1,N)\), by induction on \(j-i\) using \(c_{i,j-1}=0\), i.e. \(X_{i,j}=X_{i,j-1}+X_{i+1,j}-X_{i+1,j-1}\) (with \(X_{i+1,j-1}=0\) when it is an edge). Claim B: \(y_a=-y_{a-1}\) for \(2\le a\le N-2\), from \(c_{a,N}=0\): \(X_{a,N}+X_{1,a+1}-X_{1,a}-X_{a+1,N}=0\), and by Claim A \(X_{a,N}-X_{a+1,N}=y_a\), \(X_{1,a}-X_{1,a+1}=-y_{a-1}\). Hence \(y_m=(-1)^{m-1}u\), and by Claim A \(X_{i,j}=0\) for odd \(j-i\) and \((-1)^{i-1}u\) for even \(j-i\): \(X=-u\,v_0\).

(iii) The number of pairs equals \(\dim\cX=N(N-3)/2\) and \(\dim\ker\mathbf c=1\), so the rank is \(N(N-3)/2-1\); the space of relations is one-dimensional and contains \(\varrho\) by (i). (iv) Both sides have dimension \(N(N-3)/2-1\) and the left is contained in the right by (i). ∎

Corollary 5.2 (coordinates on a locus). For every \(S\), the map \(X\mapsto(y_t(X))_{t\in M}\) sends \(L_S\) onto \(\mathbb Y_S:=\{y\in\Q^M:\sum_{M_e}y=\sum_{M_o}y\}\) with kernel \(\Q v_0\). A linear form \(f\) on \(\cX\) vanishes on \(L_S\) if and only if \(f\in\mathrm{span}\{c_t:t\in S\}\); if \(f=\sum_t\varphi_tc_t\), this holds if and only if \(\varphi-\kappa\varrho\) is supported on \(S\) for some \(\kappa\).

Proof. The first sentence restricts Lemma 5.1(ii), (iv) to the coordinates of \(M\): for \(y\in\mathbb Y_S\) the vector \((y\text{ on }M,\ 0\text{ on }S)\) lies in \(\mathrm{im}\,\mathbf c\). The second is finite-dimensional duality (a form vanishing on \(\bigcap_{t\in S}\ker c_t\) lies in the span of the \(c_t\)), and the representations are identified by Lemma 5.1(iii). ∎

When \(\Msame=\emptyset\) the relation on \(\mathbb Y_S\) is empty; on \(\Fc_N\) both \(M_e\) and \(M_o\) are non-empty, because the set of all ee pairs is \(S_{\odds}\) and the set of all oo pairs is \(S_{\evens}\), both members of \(\cG_N\).

Lemma 5.3 (block sums and the form identity). Put \(s_{a,b}:=-c_{a,b}\) for non-adjacent legs and \(s_{a,a+1}:=X_{a,a+2}\), and \(Q_U:=\sum_{a\lt b\in U}s_{a,b}\) for a set \(U\) of legs.

  1. \(X_{i,j}=Q_{[i,j-1]}\) for every chord \((i,j)\);
  2. \(\sum_{b\ne a}s_{a,b}=0\) for every leg \(a\);
  3. \(Q_U=Q_{U^c}\) for every \(U\), and \(Q_{\text{all legs}}=0\);
  4. \(E=-Q_{\evens}=-Q_{\odds}\);
  5. for every odd chord \(P\) with sides \(A=A_P\), \(B=B_P\), \begin{equation} X_P \;=\; \sum_{t\in\rect{P}}c_t \;+\;\bigl(E-\Sigma^{ee}_B\bigr)\;-\;\Sigma^{oo}_A, \label{eq:formid} \end{equation} where \(\Sigma^{ee}_L\) and \(\Sigma^{oo}_L\) are the sums of \(c\) over the ee and the oo pairs inside \(L\).

Proof. (a) With \(G(a,b):=X_{a,b+1}\) (and \(G(a,a)=G(a+1,a)=0\)), \(s_{a,b}=G(a,b)-G(a,b-1)-G(a+1,b)+G(a+1,b-1)\) for all \(a\lt b\), adjacent pairs included (for non-adjacent pairs this is \eqref{eq:mesh}, for adjacent ones the edge convention); the double sum telescopes to \(G(i,j-1)=X_{i,j}\). (b)–(c) The same telescoping over the arcs of \(N-1\) and \(N\) legs gives \(Q_{\text{legs}\setminus a}=0=Q_{\text{all}}\); then \(\sum_{b\ne a}s_{a,b}=Q_{\text{all}}-Q_{\text{all}\setminus a}=0\), and \(Q_U=-\frac12\sum_{U\times U^c}s=Q_{U^c}\). (d) Same-parity pairs are non-adjacent, so \(Q_{\evens}=-\sum_{ee}c=-E\), and likewise for the odd legs with Lemma 5.1(i).

(e) By (a), \(X_P=Q_A\). Same-parity pairs inside \(A\) contribute \(-\Sigma^{ee}_A-\Sigma^{oo}_A\). Every \(x\in\evens(A)\) is interior to \(A\) (the ends of \(A\) are odd), so by (b) \(\sum_{y\in\odds(A)}s_{x,y}=\sum_{y\in\odds(B)}c_{x,y}+\sum_{b\ \mathrm{even}\ne x}c_{x,b}\), all these pairs being non-adjacent. Summing over \(x\in\evens(A)\), the opposite-parity part of \(Q_A\) is \(\sum_{\rect{P}}c+2\Sigma^{ee}_A+\sum_{\evens(A)\times\evens(B)}c\). In total \(Q_A=\sum_{\rect{P}}c+\Sigma^{ee}_A+\sum_{\evens(A)\times\evens(B)}c-\Sigma^{oo}_A\), and \(E=\Sigma^{ee}_A+\sum_{\evens(A)\times\evens(B)}c+\Sigma^{ee}_B\). ∎

Proposition 5.4 (flank-set dictionary). Let \(P\) be an odd chord with sides \(A,B\) and let \(S\) be arbitrary.

  1. \(\FlA=\binom{\evens(A)}{2}\cup(\evens(A)\times B)=\rect{P}\sqcup\{\text{ee pairs not inside }B\}\) and \(\FlB=\binom{\odds(B)}{2}\cup(\odds(B)\times A)=\rect{P}\sqcup\{\text{oo pairs not inside }A\}\). So \(\FlA\cap \FlB=\rect{P}\), \(\FlA\) has no oo pair, \(\FlB\) no ee pair, and \(\FlA,\FlB\in\cG_N\).
  2. \(P\in\cP_0\iff M\cap \FlA\subseteq M_e\iff M\cap \FlB\subseteq M_o\).
  3. \(\ein(P)=M_e\setminus h_P\) and \(\oin(P)=M_o\setminus h_P\), for every odd chord.

Proof. (a) The components of the circle minus \(\odds(A)\) are the singletons \(\{e\}\), \(e\in\evens(A)\), and the arc \(B\); every pair between two of them is non-adjacent. Its ee part consists of the ee pairs with a leg in \(A\), its opposite-parity part is \(\evens(A)\times\odds(B)\), and it has no oo pair. The same argument for \(\evens(B)\) gives \(\FlB\). \(|\odds(A)|=(|A|+1)/2\ge2\) and \(|\evens(B)|\ge2\), so both are \(S_T\) with \(|T|\ge2\). (b) \(M\cap \FlA\cap \FlB=M\cap\rect{P}\). (c) \(M_e\setminus \FlA\) is the set of missing ee pairs inside \(B\), and \(h_P\cap M_e=\FlA\cap M_e\) since \(\FlB\) has no ee pair; mirror for \(\oin\). ∎

Proof of Theorem 4.3. Restrict \eqref{eq:formid} to \(L_S\): every \(c_t\) with \(t\in S\) vanishes and every \(c_t\) with \(t\in M\) becomes \(y_t\). Then \(\sum_{\rect{P}}c=y(M\cap\rect{P})\); \(E-\Sigma^{ee}_B=y(M_e\setminus\inside(B))=y(M_e\cap \FlA)\); and \(\Sigma^{oo}_A=y(M_o\cap\inside(A))=y(M_o)-y(M_o\cap \FlB)=E-y(M_o\cap \FlB)\), using the relation \(y(M_o)=E\) on \(L_S\). The three sets \(M\cap\rect{P}\), \(M_e\cap \FlA\), \(M_o\cap \FlB\) are disjoint with union \(h_P\) (Proposition 5.4(a)), so \(X_P|_{L_S}=y(h_P)-E\). ∎

The identity is between functions on \(L_S\); the coefficient vector of \(X_P\) in the \(y_t\) is determined only modulo \(1_{M_e}-1_{M_o}\). A useful consequence: if \(h_Q=h_P\) then \(X_Q=X_P\) on \(L_S\).

Lemma 5.5 (the parity form). If \(E\) vanishes identically on \(L_S\), then \(S\) contains every ee pair or every oo pair. In particular \(E\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\) for every \(S\in\Fc_N\).

Proof. By Corollary 5.2, \(1_{ee}-a\) is a multiple \(\kappa\varrho\) for some \(a\) supported on \(S\). If some ee pair is not in \(S\), its coefficient gives \(\kappa=1\); then every oo pair \(u\) has \(-a_u=-1\), so \(u\in S\). The set of all ee pairs is \(S_{\odds}\in\cG_N\), and the set of all oo pairs is \(S_{\evens}\in\cG_N\). ∎

Proposition 5.6 (Proposition 4.4 in full). Let \(N\ge4\) be even. If \(S\) contains no member of \(\cG_N\), then \(S\) is non-degenerate. Hence \(S\in\Fc_N\iff M\) meets every member of \(\cG_N\), and \(\Fc_N\) is closed under subsets.

Proof. Same-parity chords never vanish on \(L_S\), since \(v_0\in L_S\). Let \(J\) be an odd chord with sides \(L,L'\), and let \(p\) be the parity of the end legs of \(L\); write \(L_p\), \(L_{1-p}\) for the legs of \(L\) of parity \(p\) and \(1-p\), and similarly for \(L'\). For \(L=A_J\) (\(p\) odd), \eqref{eq:formid} with \(E-\Sigma^{ee}_B=\Sigma^{ee}_A+\sum_{\evens(A)\times\evens(B)}c\) is the representation \[ X_J=\mathrm{rep}_L:=-\sum_{\binom{L_p}{2}}c+\sum_{\binom{L_{1-p}}{2}}c+\sum_{L_{1-p}\times L'}c, \] whose support is \(\Gamma_L:=\binom{L_p}{2}\cup\binom{L_{1-p}}{2}\cup(L_{1-p}\times L')\). Define \(\mathrm{rep}_{L'}\) by the same formula with \(L,L'\) exchanged. Comparing coefficients by pair type, \(\mathrm{rep}_L-\mathrm{rep}_{L'}=\pm\varrho\), which vanishes as a form (Lemma 5.1(i)), so \(X_J=\mathrm{rep}_{L'}\) too, and by Lemma 5.1(iii) every representation of \(X_J\) is \(\mathrm{rep}(\theta)=\theta\,\mathrm{rep}_L+(1-\theta)\mathrm{rep}_{L'}\). Its coefficients are: \(-\theta\) on parity-\(p\) pairs inside \(L\); \(\theta\) on parity-\((1-p)\) pairs inside \(L\); \(1-\theta\) on parity-\(p\) pairs inside \(L'\); \(-(1-\theta)\) on parity-\((1-p)\) pairs inside \(L'\); \(1\) on \(L_{1-p}\times L'_{p}\); \(\theta\) on \(L_{1-p}\times L'_{1-p}\); \(1-\theta\) on \(L_p\times L'_p\); \(0\) on \(L_p\times L'_{1-p}\). Hence the support of \(\mathrm{rep}(\theta)\) contains \(\Gamma_L\) if \(\theta\ne0\) and \(\Gamma_{L'}\) if \(\theta\ne1\). By Corollary 5.2, \(X_J\equiv0\) on \(L_S\) if and only if some \(\mathrm{rep}(\theta)\) is supported in \(S\), which forces \(S\supseteq\Gamma_L\) or \(S\supseteq\Gamma_{L'}\). Finally \(\Gamma_L\supseteq S_{L_p}\): cutting the circle at \(L_p\) leaves the single legs of \(L_{1-p}\) and the arc \(L'\), so \(S_{L_p}=\binom{L_{1-p}}{2}\cup(L_{1-p}\times L')\); and \(S_{L_p}\in\cG_N\) since \(|L_p|=(|L|+1)/2\ge2\). So a degenerate \(S\) contains a member of \(\cG_N\). Closure under subsets: \(M\) only grows. (At \(N=4\) there are no odd chords, and the statement is immediate.) ∎

Interpretation. In words: an odd propagator \(X_J\) can vanish identically on a coordinate locus only if \(S\) already contains the \(T\)-separated set cut at the end-parity legs of one of its sides. So non-degeneracy is free for candidate sets; this is why the theorem needs no separate hypothesis on the propagators beyond “contains no member of \(\cG_N\)”.

6. The first-order regime

This section proves that \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\) when \(\cP_1=\emptyset\). We expand the amplitude in Feynman diagrams whose propagators are the odd planar variables and whose vertices are linear forms, the Cayley parametrisation of the NLSM [KNT]. At a point of the base locus \(L_S^0\), where every same-parity invariant vanishes as well, the propagators of \(\cP_0\) are on shell and many vertices vanish. Counting orders of vanishing on the dual tree of each diagram shows that, along a line leaving the base locus into \(L_S\), the only diagram that contributes at first order is the one with no propagator at all, the \(N\)-point contact vertex, and it equals \(\pm E=\mp(\sum_{\rm even}p)^2\).

6.1 Dissections and the Cayley form

Definition 6.1 (dissections). A dissection \(D\) is a set of pairwise non-crossing odd chords. Its faces are polygons whose vertex parities alternate, so every face has an even number of vertices; \(\sum_f(|f|-2)=N-2\) and the number of faces is \(|D|+1\). A side \((u,v)\) of a face \(f\) carries the far arc of legs \([u,v-1]\): a single leg for a polygon edge, the block on the far side of the chord for a chord side. The own legs of \(f\) are the legs of its polygon-edge sides; far arcs of distinct sides are disjoint and, with the own legs, cover all legs. Each \(P\in D\) is oriented from the face on its \(B\)-side to the face on its \(A\)-side: it is an in-chord of the face on its \(A\)-side (whose far arc of \(P\) is \(B_P\)) and an out-chord of the face on its \(B\)-side (whose far arc is \(A_P\)). A face with no out-chords is a sink, one with no in-chords a source (Figure 6 shows a dissection with its faces, orientation, sink and source). The dual tree has the faces as nodes and the chords as edges. Put \(\In_f:=\mathrm{own}(f)\cup\bigcup_{R\ \mathrm{in}}B_R\), \(\Out_f:=\mathrm{own}(f)\cup\bigcup_{R\ \mathrm{out}}A_R\), and let \(U_f\) be the set of legs of the sides of \(f\) that start at an odd vertex; then \(U_f=\odds(\mathrm{own}\,f)\cup\bigcup_{R\ \mathrm{out}}A_R\).

Lemma 6.2 (minimal rectangle). For every odd chord \(P\), \(X_P-\sum_{\rect{P}}c\in\mathrm{span}\{c_t: t\ \text{same-parity}\}\). Hence \(X_P\) vanishes on \(L_S^0\) if and only if \(\rect{P}\subseteq S\), i.e. \(P\in\cP_0\).

Proof. This is \eqref{eq:formid} modulo same-parity mesh variables. For “only if”: on \(L_S^0\) the missing opposite-parity mesh variables are independent coordinates (Corollary 5.2: the only relation involves same-parity pairs only), so a combination of them vanishes there only if each coefficient does. ∎

Definition 6.3 (Cayley vertices). For a face \(f\) of a dissection put \(\mathrm{sgn}_f:=(-1)^{|f|/2-1}\) and \(V_f:=\mathrm{sgn}_f\,Q_{U_f}\) (with \(Q_U\) as in Lemma 5.3). The Cayley sum is \[ \Cay_N:=\sum_{D}\prod_{f\in\mathrm{faces}(D)}V_f\prod_{P\in D}\Bigl(-\frac1{X_P}\Bigr), \] the sum over all dissections, including \(D=\emptyset\). It is the Feynman sum with the vertices of the Cayley parametrisation [KNT, eqs. (62)–(64)]; we use it only as a defined rational function. Note \(\prod_f\mathrm{sgn}_f=(-1)^{(N-2)/2}=:\varepsilon_N\).

Theorem 6.4 (Cayley form). For every even \(N\ge4\), \(\NLSM_N=-\Cay_N\) as rational functions.

That the Cayley-parametrisation Feynman rules reproduce the NLSM is known [KNT]. We need the identity in the exact normalisation of Definition 2.2, as an identity of rational functions of the planar variables with the sign \(\varepsilon_N\), so we prove it; the idea is the standard one, that two rational functions with the same poles and residues differ by a polynomial, here a linear form, which is then killed by two zeros.

Summary of the proof (full proof: Supplement S1, Theorem S1.1.3)

(1) Mesh variables omitting one same-parity pair are linearly independent, so for \(N\ge6\) the spans of the invariants \(\{c_{1,b}\}_b\) and \(\{c_{2,b}\}_b\) meet only in 0. (2) \(\NLSM_N\) vanishes on the single-leg loci \(\cK_{\{N,2\}}=\{c_{1,b}=0\ \forall b\}\) and \(\cK_{\{1,3\}}\): the Tr\((\phi^3)\) amplitude vanishes there (a skinny causal diamond [ACDFH, §3.1.2, §3.2, §4.1]), and since \(v_0\) lies in these loci every \(\delta\)-coefficient vanishes too. (3) \(\Cay_N\) is homogeneous of degree 1, dihedrally invariant, and factorises on each odd chord as \(\Res_{X_P}\Cay_N=-\Cay_L\Cay_R\). (4) \(\Cay_N\) also vanishes on the single-leg loci, by induction on \(N\): its residues on the locus contain the factor \(\Cay\) of the piece carrying leg 1, which lies on its own single-leg locus, so the restriction is a linear form; pairing each dissection containing the chord \(X_{3,N}\) with the dissection without it cancels those terms, and an order count at a generic point shows the linear form is zero. (5) So \(\ell:=\NLSM_N+\Cay_N\) has no poles (the residues agree by Facts 2.3(b), (3) and induction), is a linear form, and vanishes on both single-leg loci; by (1) it is zero.

6.2 Vertices at the base point

For a set \(L\) of legs write \(\Sigma^{ee}_L\) and \(\Sigma^{oo}_L\) for the sums of \(c\) over the ee and the oo pairs inside \(L\).

Lemma 6.5 (face identity). For every dissection \(D\) and face \(f\), as forms on \(\cX\), \[ Q_{U_f}=\sum_{x\in U_e,\ y\in U^c_o}c_{x,y}+E-\Sigma^{ee}_{\In_f}-\Sigma^{oo}_{\Out_f}, \] with \(U_e:=\evens(U_f)=\bigcup_{R\ \mathrm{out}}\evens(A_R)\) and \(U^c_o:=\odds(U_f^c)=\bigcup_{R\ \mathrm{in}}\odds(B_R)\). Put \(\ell_f:=E-\Sigma^{ee}_{\In_f}-\Sigma^{oo}_{\Out_f}\).

  1. If every pair (out-chord \(R\), in-chord \(R'\)) of chord sides of \(f\) has a member in \(\cP_0\) (in particular if at most one chord side of \(f\) lies outside \(\cP_0\)), then \(V_f\) vanishes on \(L_S^0\) and \(V_f\equiv\mathrm{sgn}_f\,\ell_f\) on \(L_S\).
  2. If \(f\) is a sink, \(\ell_f=-\Sigma^{oo}_{\mathrm{own}\,f}\); if \(f\) is a source, \(\ell_f=-\Sigma^{ee}_{\mathrm{own}\,f}\). For \(D=\emptyset\), \(-V_{[1..N]}=\varepsilon_NE\).

Proof. Split \(Q_U\) by parity: same-parity pairs contribute \(-\Sigma^{ee}_U-\Sigma^{oo}_U\). Each \(x\in U_e\) is an even leg of some \(A_R\), hence interior to it, so \(x\pm1\in U\); by Lemma 5.3(b) \(\sum_{y\in\odds(U)}s_{x,y}=\sum_{y\in U^c_o}c_{x,y}+\sum_{b\ \mathrm{even}\ne x}c_{x,b}\). Summing over \(x\in U_e\) and collecting, \(Q_U=\sum_{U_e\times U^c_o}c+E-\Sigma^{ee}_{U^c}-\Sigma^{oo}_U\); and \(\evens(U^c)=\evens(\In_f)\), \(\odds(U)=\odds(\Out_f)\). (a) On \(L_S^0\) the same-parity parts vanish, so \(V_f|_{L_S^0}=\mathrm{sgn}_f\sum_{U_e\times U^c_o}c\). For \(R\) out and \(R'\) in, the far arcs \(A_R\) and \(B_{R'}\) are disjoint, so \(\evens(A_R)\times\odds(B_{R'})\subseteq\rect{R}\cap\rect{R'}\), which lies in \(S\) as soon as \(R\) or \(R'\) is in \(\cP_0\). So the first sum lies in \(\mathrm{span}\{c_t:t\in S\}\). (b) At a sink \(\In_f\) is all legs and \(\Out_f=\mathrm{own}\,f\); at a source \(\Out_f\) is all legs and \(\Sigma^{oo}_{\mathrm{all}}=E\). ∎

Lemma 6.6 (order count). Let \(D\not\subseteq\cP_0\), let \(k_D\) be the number of faces of \(D\) that satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma 6.5(a), and \(p_D:=|D\cap\cP_0|\). Then \(k_D\ge p_D+2\).

Proof. Delete the \(\cP_0\)-edges from the dual tree; the rest is a forest with \(p_D+1\) components. A node of degree at most 1 in the forest is a face with at most one chord side outside \(\cP_0\). A one-node component contributes one such face, a larger component (a tree) at least two, and since \(D\not\subseteq\cP_0\) some component has at least two nodes. ∎

Lemma 6.7 (sink and source walk). Let \(\emptyset\ne D\subseteq\cP_0\) and \(P\in D\). If \(\oin(P)=\emptyset\) or \(\ein(P)=\emptyset\), some face \(h\) of \(D\) has \(V_h\in\mathrm{span}\{c_t:t\in S\}\). So if \(\cP_1=\emptyset\), every non-empty \(D\subseteq\cP_0\) vanishes identically on \(L_S\).

Proof. Let \(\oin(P)=\emptyset\). Start at the face on the \(A\)-side of \(P\); while the current face has an out-chord \(R_k\), cross it to the face on its \(A\)-side. The far arcs shrink, \(A_{R_k}\subseteq A_{R_{k-1}}\subseteq\dots\subseteq A_P\), and the tree has no cycles, so the walk ends at a sink \(h\) whose own legs lie in \(A_P\). All chord sides of \(h\) are in \(\cP_0\), so by Lemma 6.5(a),(b), \(V_h\equiv-\mathrm{sgn}_h\Sigma^{oo}_{\mathrm{own}\,h}\) on \(L_S\), and every oo pair inside \(A_P\) is in \(S\). The case \(\ein(P)=\emptyset\) walks backwards to a source with own legs in \(B_P\). ∎

6.3 The first-order term

Theorem 6.8 (first-order term). Let \(N\ge4\) be even and \(S\) non-degenerate.

  1. For generic \(x_0\in L_S^0\) and generic \(y\in L_S\), \(\NLSM_N(x_0+z\,y)=z\,G_S(y)+O(z^2)\), where \[ G_S=\varepsilon_NE-\sum_{\emptyset\ne D\subseteq\cP_1\ \text{non-crossing}}\ \prod_{f}V_f\prod_{P\in D}\Bigl(-\frac1{X_P}\Bigr), \] restricted to \(L_S\), a rational function of the missing same-parity mesh variables alone. (\(\NLSM_N\) itself is in general undefined at \(x_0\), where the chords of \(\cP_0\) vanish.)
  2. If \(\cP_1=\emptyset\) and \(S\) contains neither all ee pairs nor all oo pairs (in particular if \(S\in\Fc_N\)), then \(G_S=\varepsilon_NE\ne0\) and \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\).

Here \(\varepsilon_N=(-1)^{N/2+1}\) for \(\NLSM_N\); for \(\cA_N\) the constant is \(+1\) at every \(N\). Part (a) is not used in the proof of (b); it is the formula behind Remark 6.9 and the open question of §10.

Proof. Expand \(\NLSM_N=-\Cay_N\) (Theorem 6.4) and evaluate each diagram along \(x_0+zy\). Every \(V_f\) and \(X_P\) is linear, so it equals \(z\,V(y)\) if \(V\) vanishes on \(L_S^0\) and \(V(x_0)+zV(y)\) otherwise. By Lemma 6.6 a dissection \(D\not\subseteq\cP_0\) contributes \(O(z^{k_D-p_D})=O(z^2)\); a non-empty \(D\subseteq\cP_0\) contributes exactly \(z\) times its value at \(y\) (every factor vanishes at \(x_0\) and #faces − #chords = 1), and it vanishes identically on \(L_S\) unless \(D\subseteq\cP_1\) (Lemma 6.7); \(D=\emptyset\) gives \(-V_{[1..N]}(x_0+zy)=\varepsilon_NE(x_0+zy)=z\,\varepsilon_NE(y)\), as \(E\) vanishes on \(L_S^0\). This is (a).

For (b) we write the argument without “generic” and without Laurent series. (1) The forms \(X_P\), \(P\) odd, \(P\notin\cP_0\), do not vanish identically on \(L_S^0\) (Lemma 6.2); choose \(x_0\in L_S^0\) avoiding all of them (a vector space over an infinite field is not a finite union of proper subspaces). (2) Choose \(y\in L_S\) with \(X_P(y)\ne0\) for every odd \(P\) (non-degeneracy) and \(E(y)\ne0\) (Lemma 5.5). (3) \(\mathrm{Den}(z):=\prod_{P\ \mathrm{odd}}X_P(x_0+zy)=z^{|\cP_0|}u(z)\) with \(u(0)\ne0\), and \(\mathrm{Num}(z):=-\mathrm{Den}(z)\,\Cay_N(x_0+zy)\) is a polynomial. (4) For \(D\not\subseteq\cP_0\) the \(D\)-term of \(\mathrm{Num}\) is divisible by \(z^{|\cP_0|+2}\) (Lemma 6.6); for \(\emptyset\ne D\subseteq\cP_0\) it is \(0\) (Lemma 6.7, as \(\cP_1=\emptyset\)); the \(D=\emptyset\) term is \(\varepsilon_NE(y)\,z\,\mathrm{Den}(z)\). So \(\mathrm{Num}(z)\equiv\varepsilon_NE(y)u(0)\,z^{|\cP_0|+1}\bmod z^{|\cP_0|+2}\), and \(\mathrm{Num}\ne0\). (5) Pick \(z_0\) with \(\mathrm{Num}(z_0)\ne0\ne\mathrm{Den}(z_0)\); at \(x:=x_0+z_0y\in L_S\) every odd chord is non-zero and \(\NLSM_N(x)=\mathrm{Num}(z_0)/\mathrm{Den}(z_0)\ne0\). ∎

Membership in \(\Fc_N\) is not needed for (b) beyond the two whole-parity exclusions; non-degeneracy and \(\cP_1=\emptyset\) suffice. If \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) has no pole, it is a linear form (homogeneity of degree 1) and equals \(G_S\).

Remark 6.9 (the first regime is needed). The case \(\cP_1=\emptyset\) cannot be absorbed into the residue argument: there are maximal \(S\in\Fc_N\) with \(\cP_1=\emptyset\) on which \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) has no pole at all and equals \(\varepsilon_NE\), so no residue can witness non-vanishing. This was found by computation at \(N=8\) and \(N=10\): among the 66 (resp. 2 227) dihedral orbits of maximal candidate sets, 43 (resp. 1 129) have \(\cP_1=\emptyset\), and 23 (resp. 378) of these are pole-free, while every orbit with \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\) has a pole (Supplement S4). A single statement that would make the two regimes one, that the first-order term is non-zero on every candidate set, is open (§10).

7. The residue regime

Throughout this section \(N\ge6\) is even, \(S\) is a set of pairs with missing set \(M\), and \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\). Unless stated, \(S\) is arbitrary; where \(S\in\Fc_N\) is used, the statement says so. A pair is clean if it is not missing.

The plan is physical. On \(L_S\) the chords of one flank class all restrict to the same linear form \(x\), so a propagator \(1/x\) appears in many diagrams at once, and the amplitude can have a pole along \(x=0\) even though no single channel is singled out. We show that the pole is simple and that its residue is a product of two lower-point amplitudes, the two ends of the nested chain of channels (Figure 7), with everything in between collapsing to a sign. For this to feed the induction, the two lower-point loci must again be candidate sets; that is what the following notion controls.

7.1 Separators and the innermost chord

Why separators. A lower-point amplitude on the side \(L\) of a chord sees the invariants inside \(L\), and the new leg that replaces the far side. A member \(S_G\) of \(\cG\) at the lower-point level that uses the new leg as a cut leg pulls back to a set of pairs inside \(L\) separated by the rest of \(G\), a separator of \(L\). So the child is a candidate set exactly when \(M\) meets every such separated set (Proposition 7.18(3)); we call such a side linked. The top of the chain needs a linked even-ended side, the bottom a linked odd-ended side, and these asymmetric roles are why the argument looks for an innermost chord and then for a bottom.

Definition 7.1. For an arc \(L\) of legs, \(\intr L\) is \(L\) without its two end legs and \(\inside(L)\) the set of pairs with both legs in \(L\). Let \(P\) be an odd chord and \(L\in\{A_P,B_P\}\); the separator parity \(q\) of \(L\) is even for \(A_P\) and odd for \(B_P\).

  • An admissible separator of \(L\) is \(\Theta=\{x\}\) with \(x\in\intr L\) of either parity, or a set \(\Theta\subseteq\intr L\) of at least two legs of parity \(q\). The runs of \(L\setminus\Theta\) are its maximal sub-arcs, and \(\sep_L(\Theta)\) is the set of pairs inside \(L\), with no leg in \(\Theta\), whose legs lie in different runs.
  • \(\Theta\) is an \(M\)-free separator if \(\sep_L(\Theta)\cap M=\emptyset\) (a “free cut”). \(L\) is linked if no admissible separator of \(L\) is \(M\)-free.
  • \(\cS_B:=\{P\in\cP_1: B_P\ \text{linked}\}\). An innermost chord is a \(P^*\in\cS_B\) such that no chord of \(\cS_B\) has its odd-ended side strictly inside \(A^*:=A_{P^*}\). (It need not be unique; any one serves.) \(B^*:=B_{P^*}\).
  • The flank class of \(P\in\cP_1\) is \(\{Q\in\cP_1:h_Q=h_P\}\); for chords of \(\cP_0\) this is the same as \(\ein(Q)=\ein(P)\) and \(\oin(Q)=\oin(P)\). A member \(t\) of a class \(K\) is its top if \(A_Q\subseteq A_t\) for all \(Q\in K\), and \(b\) is its bottom if \(A_b\subseteq A_Q\) for all \(Q\in K\). \(K^*\) is the flank class of \(P^*\).

If \(S_G\in\cG_N\) meets \(L\) in an admissible separator \(\Theta\) (\(G\cap L=\Theta\) and \(G\not\subseteq L\)), then \(S_G\cap\inside(L)=\sep_L(\Theta)\), and every admissible separator arises this way.

Lemma 7.2 (descent to a smaller side). Let \(P\in\cP_1\). (A) If \(A_P\) is not linked, some \(Q\in\cP_1\) has \(A_Q\subsetneq A_P\). (B) If \(B_P\) is not linked, some \(Q\in\cP_1\) has \(B_Q\subsetneq B_P\). No hypothesis on \(S\) is needed.

Proof. (A) Write \(A=A_P=[a,a']\) and let \(\Theta\) be an \(M\)-free admissible separator of \(A\); pick \(f\in\oin(P)\). Case 1: \(\Theta\) is even. The legs of \(f\) are odd, so \(f\) avoids \(\Theta\) and, not lying in \(\sep_A(\Theta)\), lies inside one run \(R\). Runs end at \(a\), \(a'\) or next to an even leg of \(\Theta\), so \(R\) is odd-ended, of odd length \(\ge3\), and \(R\subsetneq A\). Let \(Q\) be the odd chord with \(A_Q=R\). Then \(f\in\oin(Q)\), \(\ein(Q)\supseteq\ein(P)\ne\emptyset\) (as \(B_Q\supseteq B_P\)), and \(Q\in\cP_0\): \(\rect{Q}=[\evens(R)\times\odds(B_P)]\cup [\evens(R)\times\odds(A\setminus R)]\); the first block lies in \(\rect{P}\subseteq S\), and a pair of the second block has legs in different runs, neither in \(\Theta\) (the odd leg is not in the even \(\Theta\)), so it lies in \(\sep_A(\Theta)\) and is not missing. Case 2: \(\Theta=\{x\}\), \(x\) odd. The pieces \([a,x]\) and \([x,a']\) are odd-ended of odd length \(\ge3\); \(f\) lies inside one of them, \(R\), and the chord \(Q\) with \(A_Q=R\) is in \(\cP_1\) by the same argument (a pair \((e,o)\) with \(e\in\evens(R)\), \(o\) odd on the other side of \(x\), is in \(\sep_A(\{x\})\)). (B) is the mirror, with \(g\in\ein(P)\), odd separators, and \(Q\) the chord with \(B_Q=R\). ∎

It matters that linkedness counts missing opposite-parity pairs as well: with same-parity pairs only, the second block of \(\rect{Q}\) could be missing.

Corollary 7.3 (innermost chords exist). If \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\) then \(\cS_B\ne\emptyset\), and an innermost chord exists.

Proof. Take \(P\in\cP_1\) with \(|B_P|\) minimal; by Lemma 7.2(B), \(P\in\cS_B\). Then take \(P^*\in\cS_B\) with \(|A_{P^*}|\) minimal. ∎

Lemma 7.4 (containment). Let \(P\in\cP_1\) with \(B_P\) linked, and \(Q\in\cP_0\) with \(\ein(Q)\supseteq\ein(P)\) and \(A_Q\cap A_P\ne\emptyset\). Then \(A_Q\subseteq A_P\). Mirror: if \(P\in\cP_1\) has \(A_P\) linked, \(Q\in\cP_0\) has \(\oin(Q)\supseteq\oin(P)\) and \(B_Q\cap B_P\ne\emptyset\), then \(A_P\subseteq A_Q\). No hypothesis on \(S\).

Proof. Suppose \(A_Q\not\subseteq A_P\). A pair of \(\ein(P)\) lies inside \(B_P\) and (being in \(\ein(Q)\)) inside \(B_Q\), so \(B_P\cap B_Q\ne\emptyset\); with \(A_P\cap A_Q\ne\emptyset\) this makes \(A_P\cup A_Q\) an arc and \(B_P\cap B_Q\) one non-empty arc. \(\Delta:=A_Q\setminus A_P\) is one or two arcs, each running from an even end leg of \(B_P\) to an odd end leg of \(A_Q\), so of even length \(\ge2\). \(\Theta:=\odds(\Delta)\) is a non-empty set of odd interior legs of \(B_P\), hence admissible. The runs of \(B_P\setminus\Theta\) are the even legs of \(\Delta\) (singletons) and the arc \(B_P\cap B_Q\). A pair of \(\sep_{B_P}(\Theta)\) has a leg \(e\in\evens(\Delta)\subseteq\evens(A_Q)\); if its other leg \(y\) is odd, \(y\in\odds(B_Q)\) and the pair is in \(\rect{Q}\subseteq S\); if \(y\) is even, the pair is an ee pair inside \(B_P\), and if missing it would lie in \(\ein(P)\subseteq\ein(Q)\), inside \(B_Q\), but \(e\in A_Q\). So \(\Theta\) is \(M\)-free, against \(B_P\) being linked. The mirror follows by the rotation by one leg, which exchanges the two sides of every odd chord and the two parities. ∎

Proposition 7.5 (top and bottom). Let \(P^*\in\cS_B\) and \(K^*\) its flank class. (a) \(P^*\) is the top of \(K^*\). (b) If \(b\in K^*\) has \(A_b\) linked, then \(b\) is the unique bottom of \(K^*\). Hence the statement

(Bot)  \(K^*\) has a member \(b\) with \(A_b\) linked

implies that \(K^*\) has a top and a bottom. No hypothesis on \(S\).

Proof. (a) For \(Q\in K^*\), \(\ein(Q)=\ein(P^*)\), and \(A_Q\cap A^*\ne\emptyset\) because a pair of \(\oin(Q)=\oin(P^*)\ne\emptyset\) is inside both; Lemma 7.4 gives \(A_Q\subseteq A^*\). (b) The mirror of Lemma 7.4 with \(P=b\): \(B_Q\cap B_b\ne\emptyset\) because a pair of \(\ein(Q)=\ein(b)\) is inside both, so \(A_b\subseteq A_Q\). Two bottoms have equal odd-ended sides and are equal. ∎

That (Bot) holds at an innermost chord when \(S\in\Fc_N\) is the content of §8 (Proposition 8.15).

7.2 Proportional odd chords

The residue step needs the pole class to be the full proportionality class of \(X_{P^*}\) among all odd chords, every member at \(+X_{P^*}\); otherwise a chord outside the class with restriction proportional to \(X_{P^*}\) would leave a pole of the complementary part on the same hyperplane.

Lemma 7.6 (proportionality). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\) and \(P\in\cP_1\). An odd chord \(Q\) satisfies \(X_Q=\lambda X_P\) on \(L_S\) for some \(\lambda\ne0\) if and only if \(h_Q=h_P\); and then \(\lambda=+1\) and \(Q\in\cP_1\).

Proof. (0) Linear algebra. By Theorem 4.3 and Corollary 5.2, with \(p:=1_{h_P}\), \(q:=1_{h_Q}\) and \(\varrho:=1_{M_e}-1_{M_o}\) (vectors on \(M\)), the hypothesis reads \(q-1_{M_e}=\lambda(p-1_{M_e})+\nu\varrho\) for some \(\nu\in\Q\). Coordinatewise: on \(\Mix\), \(q_t=\lambda p_t\); on \(M_e\), \(q_t-1=\lambda(p_t-1)+\nu\); on \(M_o\), \(q_t=\lambda p_t-\nu\).

(1) The values of \(p\). By Proposition 5.4, \(P\in\cP_0\) gives \(p=0\) on \(\Mix\), \(M\cap\FlA_P\subseteq M_e\) and \(M\cap\FlB_P\subseteq M_o\). \(\FlA_P,\FlB_P\in\cG_N\) and \(S\in\Fc_N\), so \(M\) meets both (first use of \(\Fc\)): \(p\) takes the value 1 at a point of \(M_e\) and at a point of \(M_o\). \(P\in\cP_1\) gives \(\ein(P)=M_e\setminus h_P\ne\emptyset\) and \(\oin(P)=M_o\setminus h_P\ne\emptyset\): \(p\) takes the value 0 at a point of \(M_e\) and at a point of \(M_o\).

(2) Only two solutions. At a point of \(M_o\) with \(p_t=0\), \(q_t=-\nu\in\{0,1\}\), so \(\nu\in\{0,-1\}\). If \(\nu=0\): at a point of \(M_e\) with \(p_t=0\), \(q_t=1-\lambda\in\{0,1\}\) and \(\lambda\ne0\), so \(\lambda=1\); then \(q=p\) on \(M_e\), on \(M_o\) and on \(\Mix\) (where \(q=\lambda p=0\)), so \(h_Q=h_P\). If \(\nu=-1\): on \(M_e\), \(p_t=1\) gives \(q_t=0\) and \(p_t=0\) gives \(q_t=-\lambda\), so \(\lambda=-1\) and \(q_t=1\); on \(M_o\), \(p_t=0\) gives \(q_t=1\) and \(p_t=1\) gives \(q_t=0\); on \(\Mix\), \(q=0\). So \(h_Q=\Msame\setminus h_P\) and \(\lambda=-1\). Conversely \(h_Q=h_P\) gives \(X_Q=X_P\). (\(\lambda=0\) cannot occur, by non-degeneracy.)

(3) The case \(\lambda=-1\) is empty. Suppose \(h_Q=\Msame\setminus h_P\). Then (i) \(M\cap\rect{Q}=h_Q\cap\Mix=\emptyset\), so \(Q\in\cP_0\) and \(\rect{P},\rect{Q}\subseteq S\); (ii) since \(h_R\cap M_e\) is the set of missing ee pairs not inside \(B_R\) and \(h_R\cap M_o\) the set of missing oo pairs not inside \(A_R\), every missing ee pair lies inside exactly one of \(B_P,B_Q\) and every missing oo pair inside exactly one of \(A_P,A_Q\); (iii) \(\ein(P)\ne\emptyset\) and \(\ein(Q)=M_e\cap h_P\ne\emptyset\) by (1). From (i)–(iii) we produce a member of \(\cG_N\) that \(M\) misses (second use of \(\Fc\)).

Odd-ended sides are not nested: \(A_Q\subseteq A_P\) gives \(B_P\subseteq B_Q\), so a pair of \(\ein(P)\) is inside both even-ended sides, against (ii); \(A_P\subseteq A_Q\) likewise contradicts \(\ein(Q)\ne\emptyset\). Two local facts: (loc-e) an even leg of \(J:=A_P\cap A_Q\) carries no missing pair except opposite-parity pairs to odd legs of \(J\) (an ee pair at it is inside no even-ended side, so not missing by (ii); an opposite-parity pair to an odd leg of \(B_P\) or \(B_Q\) is in \(\rect{P}\) or \(\rect{Q}\)); (loc-o) mirror for odd legs of \(J':=B_P\cap B_Q\). Cases. Two non-nested odd-ended arcs meet in \(\emptyset\), one arc or two arcs, and cannot abut (the leg after an odd end is even; an odd-ended side starts at an odd leg).

  1. \(J=\emptyset\): \(J'\) is two non-empty even-ended arcs \(J'_1,J'_2\); take \(T:=\evens(J'_1)\cup\evens(J'_2)\). The components of the circle minus \(T\) are the odd legs of \(J'\) (clean by (loc-o)), \(A_P\) and \(A_Q\). An \(A_P\)–\(A_Q\) pair is inside no side, so not a missing ee or oo pair by (ii), and if opposite-parity it lies in \(\evens(A_P)\times\odds(A_Q)\subseteq\rect{P}\) or \(\evens(A_Q)\times\odds(A_P)\subseteq\rect{Q}\).
  2. \(J\) one arc (odd-ended), \(J'\) one non-empty even-ended arc. (2a) \(|\odds(J)|\ge2\): \(T:=\odds(J)\); components: the even legs of \(J\) (clean by (loc-e)) and the rest. (2b) \(J=\{j\}\), \(|J'|\ge3\): \(T:=\evens(J')\); components: the odd legs of \(J'\) and the rest. (2c) \(J=\{j\}\), \(J'=\{y\}\): \(T:=\{j,y\}\), a non-adjacent opposite-parity pair, so \(S_T\) is an odd-chord diamond; components \(I=A_P\setminus A_Q\) and \(I'=A_Q\setminus A_P\); an \(I\)–\(I'\) pair is inside no side, and \(\evens(I)\times\odds(I')\subseteq\rect{P}\), \(\evens(I')\times\odds(I)\subseteq\rect{Q}\).
  3. \(J\) two arcs \(J_1,J_2\) (\(J'=\emptyset\)): \(T:=\odds(J_1)\cup\odds(J_2)\); components: the even legs of \(J\) (clean) and \(B_P\), \(B_Q\); a \(B_P\)–\(B_Q\) pair is inside no side, and \(\evens(B_P)\times\odds(B_Q)\subseteq\rect{Q}\), \(\evens(B_Q)\times\odds(B_P)\subseteq\rect{P}\).

In every case \(S_T\in\cG_N\) and \(S_T\cap M=\emptyset\), a contradiction. Membership in \(\cP_1\): if \(h_Q=h_P\) then \(M\cap\rect{Q}=h_Q\cap\Mix=\emptyset\), so \(Q\in\cP_0\), and \(\ein(Q)=\ein(P)\), \(\oin(Q)=\oin(P)\) are non-empty. ∎

Consequence. The proportionality class of \(X_{P^*}\) among all odd chords is \(K^*\subseteq\cP_1\), every member at \(+X_{P^*}\); same-parity chords carry no pole of \(\NLSM_N\). With Proposition 7.5 this is the hypothesis of the residue theorem below, and the bottom \(b\) lies in \(\cP_1\), which Proposition 7.18 needs.

Remark (not used). If neither chord is in \(\cP_1\), proportional pairs with different missing flank sets occur. For \(S\in\Fc_N\), odd chords \(P,Q\) satisfy \(X_Q=\lambda X_P\) on \(L_S\) with \(\lambda\ne0\) if and only if \(\lambda=1\) and either \(h_Q=h_P\), or both chords lie outside \(\cP_0\), \(M\cap\rect{P}=M\cap\rect{Q}\ne\emptyset\) and \(\{(\ein(P),\oin(P)),(\ein(Q),\oin(Q))\}=\{(\emptyset,M_o),(M_e,\emptyset)\}\). The proof of clause 1 never uses this classification, and we omit its proof.

7.3 The class residue

Conventions on rational functions. Let \(V\) be a linear subspace and \(x\) a non-zero linear form on \(V\), \(H:=\{x=0\}\). A rational function \(f=F/D\) (with \(D\) a product of linear forms not vanishing identically on \(V\)) restricts to \(V\); it is regular at generic points of \(H\) if it can be written with \(x\nmid D|_V\), and then \(f|_H\) is defined; it has at most a simple pole along \(H\) if \(x f\) is regular there, and its residue is \((xf)|_H\). We use the standard facts: (RF1) a polynomial vanishing on \(H\) is divisible by \(x\); (RF2) a polynomial divisible by pairwise non-proportional non-zero linear forms is divisible by their product; (RF3) a rational function whose denominator is a product of pairwise non-proportional linear forms and which is regular at generic points of each of their hyperplanes is a polynomial; (RF4) a polynomial homogeneous of degree 0 is constant; (RF5) regularity and values along \(H\) pull back under linear maps whose image is not contained in the pole locus.

Sub-polygons. A sub-polygon \(r\) of the \(N\)-gon is given by a cyclically ordered set of \(n_r\ge3\) dual points; its sides are edges or chords of the \(N\)-gon, its diagonals are chords, and its planar variables are the parent \(X\) of its diagonals. It is alternating if consecutive vertices have different parity (every side is an edge or an odd chord); then \(n_r\) is even and \(v_0\) restricts to \(\pm\) the \(v_0\) of \(r\). For a set \(K\) of odd chords let \(K_r:=K\cap\mathrm{diagonals}(r)\), \(A^{\neg K}_r:=\sum_{\TT\cap K_r=\emptyset}\prod_{d\in\TT}1/X_d\) (triangulations of \(r\) avoiding \(K\)), \(N^{\neg K}_r\) its coefficient of \(\delta^{-(n_r-2)}\) after the \(\delta\)-shift, and \(N_r:=N^{\neg\emptyset}_r=\NLSM_{n_r}\) of \(r\).

Lemma 7.7 (grouping). Let \(r\) be alternating and \(K\) a set of odd chords.

  1. \(A^{\neg K}_r(X+\delta v_0)=O(\delta^{-(n_r-2)})\) and \(N^{\neg K}_r=\sum_{D'\subseteq K_r\ \text{non-crossing}}(-1)^{|D'|}\prod_{P\in D'}X_P^{-1} \prod_{f}N_f\), the product over the faces of \(D'\) in \(r\). The only denominators of \(N^{\neg K}_r\) are odd diagonals of \(r\) outside \(K\), each simple.
  2. \(N_r=\sum_{D\subseteq K_r\ \text{non-crossing}}\ \prod_{P\in D}X_P^{-1}\prod_{f}N^{\neg K}_f\). For \(K=\{C\}\): \(N_r=N^{\neg C}_r+N_LN_R/X_C\).

Proof. (a) For a triangulation \(\TT\), \(\sum_{D'\subseteq\TT\cap K_r}(-1)^{|D'|}=[\TT\cap K_r=\emptyset]\); exchanging sums, and using that the triangulations of \(r\) containing \(D'\) are the tuples of triangulations of the faces of \(D'\), \(A^{\neg K}_r=\sum_{D'}(-1)^{|D'|}\prod X_P^{-1}\prod_fA_f\). The chords of \(K\) are odd, hence unshifted; each face is alternating, so \(A_f(X+\delta v_0)=O(\delta^{-(n_f-2)})\) by Facts 2.3(a); and \(\sum_f(n_f-2)=n_r-2\). Take the \(\delta^{-(n_r-2)}\) coefficient. (b) Group the triangulations of \(r\) by \(D=\TT\cap K_r\), a non-crossing set; triangulations with \(\TT\cap K=D\) are tuples of \(K\)-free triangulations of the faces, so \(A_r=\sum_D\prod_{P\in D}X_P^{-1}\prod_fA^{\neg K}_f\) exactly; take the leading coefficient with (a). ∎

Definition 7.8 (hypotheses of the residue theorem). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\), let \(K\) be a set of odd chords and \(x:=X_{P}|_{L_S}\) for some \(P\in K\). Assume

  • (H1) no odd chord vanishes identically on \(L_S\);
  • (H2) for every odd chord \(Q\): \(Q\in K\) iff \(X_Q|_{L_S}\in\Q^\times x\); and \(X_Q|_{L_S}=x\) for every \(Q\in K\);
  • (H3) \(K\subseteq\cP_1\) and all members of \(K\) have the same missing flank set;
  • (H4) \(K\) has a top \(t\) and a bottom \(b\).

Put \(H:=L_S\cap\{x=0\}\) and \(d:=(|A_t|-|A_b|)/2\). The side amplitudes are \(a_t:=\NLSM_{|B_t|+1}\) of the sub-polygon bounding \(B_t\) and closed by the chord \(t\) (the B-child of \(t\)), and \(b_b:=\NLSM_{|A_b|+1}\) of the sub-polygon bounding \(A_b\) and closed by \(b\) (the A-child of \(b\)), both as functions of the parent planar variables.

By (H2), a linear form vanishing on \(H\) is a multiple of \(x\), so no odd chord outside \(K\) vanishes on \(H\). For \(K=K^*\) all four hypotheses hold: (H1) by Proposition 4.4, (H2) and (H3) by Lemma 7.6, (H4) by Proposition 7.5 once (Bot) is known.

Theorem 7.9 (class residue). Under (H1)–(H4), \(x\cdot\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) is regular at generic points of \(H\) and \begin{equation} \bigl(x\cdot\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\bigr)\big|_H=(-1)^d\,a_t|_H\cdot b_b|_H. \label{eq:res} \end{equation} So \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) has at most a simple pole along \(H\). In the normalisation \(\cA_n=(-1)^{n/2-1}\NLSM_n\) the sign drops out: \((x\cdot\cA_N)|_H=\cA(B\text{-child of }t)\cdot\cA(A\text{-child of }b)\).

Interpretation (physical meaning). Ordinary factorisation (Facts 2.3(b)) is the residue on one channel \(X_C=0\). Here all channels of the class \(K\) are the same hyperplane of \(L_S\), so the residue collects every chain of nested channels \(t=Q_0\succ Q_1\succ\dots\succ Q_k=b\) at once. The regions between consecutive channels are lower-point amplitudes of the strip legs, whose momenta obey the orthogonality relations that \(S\) imposes (Lemma 7.12); on \(H\) only the term whose ends are the top \(t\) and the bottom \(b\) survives (Lemma 7.15). Every chain from \(t\) to \(b\) survives inside that term's factor \(\varphi(t,b)\) (Lemma 7.11), and the sum over these chains of the products of region amplitudes is \((-1)^d\) in total (Theorem 7.17). The outcome looks like factorisation on a single channel whose two sides are the polygon beyond \(t\) and the polygon inside \(b\): the \(2d\) strip legs disappear from the residue. Figure 7 shows the geometry; §10 compares this with the splits of [CEU, CDHS].

The \(\cA\) form. The children have \(n_B=|B_t|+1\) and \(n_A=|A_b|+1\) legs, and \(n_B+n_A=N+2-2d\). So the exponents \((N/2-1)+d\) and \((n_B+n_A)/2-2=N/2-1-d\) differ by \(2d\).

The side amplitudes are the full children. No chord of \(K\) is a diagonal of the B-side polygon of \(t\): its odd-ended side cannot lie in \(B_t\) (it contains \(A_b\subseteq A_t\)), and its even-ended side lies in \(B_t\) only if its odd-ended side contains \(A_t\), which the top forbids unless it is \(t\). The mirror holds for the A-side of \(b\). On \(H\), \(X_t=x=0\), so the closing side is massless.

7.4 Proof of the residue theorem

The proof has three parts: an exact identity that isolates the \(K\)-poles (Lemma 7.11), the vanishing of all but one term on \(H\) (Lemma 7.15), and the value \((-1)^d\) of the surviving strip factor (Theorem 7.17). For \(Q\succeq Q'\) in \(K\) (meaning \(A_Q\supseteq A_{Q'}\)) let \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) be the sub-polygon with vertices \(a,a+1,\dots,c,\;c'+1,\dots,a'+1\), where \(A_Q=[a,a']\), \(A_{Q'}=[c,c']\): its legs are the two strips \(\St(Q,Q'):=A_Q\setminus A_{Q'}\) (two arcs, each of even length), the side \(\ell_A=(c,c'+1)\) (the chord \(Q'\)) and the side \(\ell_B=(a'+1,a)\) (the chord \(Q\)); it is alternating. Put \(\varphi(Q,Q):=1\), \(\varphi(Q,Q'):=N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}/x\) for \(Q\succ Q'\), \(a_Q:=N^{\neg K}\) of the B-side polygon of \(Q\), \(b_Q:=N^{\neg K}\) of its A-side polygon, and \(N^{\neg K}\) that of the whole \(N\)-gon.

Lemma 7.10 (chains). Under (H4), two distinct members of \(K\) do not cross if and only if their odd-ended sides are strictly nested. So the non-crossing subsets of \(K\) are the chains of \(\succ\).

Proof. Two distinct chords do not cross iff a side of one contains a side of the other: \(A_Q\subseteq A_{Q'}\), \(A_{Q'}\subseteq A_Q\), \(A_Q\cap A_{Q'}=\emptyset\), or \(A_Q\cup A_{Q'}\) is everything. All odd-ended sides contain \(A_b\ne\emptyset\) and lie in \(A_t\ne\) all legs. ∎

Lemma 7.11 (collapse identity). Under (H1), (H2), (H4), on \(L_S\): \[ x\cdot\NLSM_N=x\cdot N^{\neg K}+\sum_{Q\succeq Q'\ \text{in}\ K}\varphi(Q,Q')\,a_Q\,b_{Q'}, \] and \(N^{\neg K}\), \(a_Q\), \(b_Q\) are regular at generic points of \(H\).

Proof. Apply Lemma 7.7(b) to the \(N\)-gon and \(K\). By Lemma 7.10 a non-crossing \(D\) is a chain \(Q_1\succ\dots\succ Q_s\); its faces are the B-side polygon of \(Q_1\), the regions \(\Omega(Q_i,Q_{i+1})\) and the A-side polygon of \(Q_s\); on \(L_S\) every \(X_P\), \(P\in K\), equals \(x\). The \(K\)-diagonals of \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) are exactly the members strictly between \(Q\) and \(Q'\) (by Lemma 7.10), so Lemma 7.7(b) inside \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) gives \(\sum_{\text{chains }Q=c_0\succ\dots\succ c_s=Q'}x^{-(s-1)}\prod_iN^{\neg K}_{\Omega(c_i,c_{i+1})}=N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}\). Hence the chains with top \(Q\) and bottom \(Q'\) sum to \(x^{-1}a_Q\,x^{-1}N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}\,b_{Q'}\) (and to \(x^{-1}a_Qb_Q\) if \(Q=Q'\)), and \(D=\emptyset\) gives \(N^{\neg K}\). Multiply by \(x\). Regularity: by Lemma 7.7(a) the denominators are odd chords outside \(K\), which do not vanish on \(H\). ∎

So if (E) \(a_Q|_H=0\) for \(Q\ne t\) and \(b_Q|_H=0\) for \(Q\ne b\), and every \(\varphi(Q,Q')\) is regular at generic points of \(H\), then \((x\NLSM_N)|_H=\varphi(t,b)|_H\,a_t|_H\,b_b|_H\). It remains to prove (E) and \(\varphi(t,b)|_H=(-1)^d\).

Lemma 7.12 (strip regions). Let \(Q\succ Q'\) be chords of \(\cP_0\) with \(h_Q=h_{Q'}\). On \(L_S\), writing the mesh variables of \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) through momenta (\(c=-2p\cdot p\) for non-adjacent legs): (i) every same-parity mesh variable of \(\Omega(Q,Q')\) vanishes; (ii) \(\ell_A,\ell_B\) sit at positions of opposite parity in \(\Omega\), and \(X_Q=X_{Q'}\); (iii) each odd strip leg is orthogonal to each leg of \(A_{Q'}\), and each even strip leg to each leg of \(B_Q\).

Proof. \(h_Q=h_{Q'}\) gives \(\oin(Q)=\oin(Q')\) and \(\ein(Q)=\ein(Q')\). (1) A missing oo pair inside \(A_Q\) meeting \(\St(Q,Q')\) would be in \(\oin(Q)=\oin(Q')\), inside \(A_{Q'}\): impossible. (2) A missing ee pair inside \(B_{Q'}=\St(Q,Q')\sqcup B_Q\) meeting \(\St(Q,Q')\) would be in \(\ein(Q)\), inside \(B_Q\): impossible. (3) Pairs (even leg of \(A_{Q'}\), odd leg of \(\St(Q,Q')\)) lie in \(\rect{Q'}\subseteq S\). (4) Pairs (even leg of \(\St(Q,Q')\), odd leg of \(B_Q\)) lie in \(\rect{Q}\subseteq S\). Around \(\Omega\) the strip \([a,c-1]\) runs odd…even, then \(\ell_A\), then \([c'+1,a']\) even…odd, then \(\ell_B\). Same-parity strip pairs are parent pairs, zero by (1), (2). For an odd strip leg \(u\), the mesh variable of \((u,\ell_A)\) is \(\sum_{w\in A_{Q'}}c_{u,w}\), zero by (1), (3); for an even strip leg, that of \((u,\ell_B)\) is \(\sum_{w\in B_Q}c_{u,w}\), zero by (2), (4). This gives (i), (iii). (ii): the odd-position momenta of \(\Omega\) sum to \(p_{\rm odd}\) and the even-position ones to \(p_{\rm even}=-p_{\rm odd}\); \(p_{\rm odd}^2=X_{Q'}+\sum2p\cdot p\) over odd-position pairs, all non-adjacent and hence zero; so \(p_{\rm odd}^2=X_{Q'}\) and likewise \(p_{\rm even}^2=X_Q\). ∎

Lemma 7.13 (corners). For a polygon with side values \(X_{k,k+1}=m_k\), the massless-form mesh variable \(c^{\mathrm{ml}}_{i,j}\) (every side set to 0) satisfies \(c^{\mathrm{ml}}_{k-1,k+1}=c_{k-1,k+1}+m_k\) and \(c^{\mathrm{ml}}_{i,j}=c_{i,j}\) otherwise. (Sub-polygons such as the regions \(\Omega\) have massive sides: a side that is a chord of the parent carries the parent planar variable of that chord.)

Proof. The only side variable that can occur in \(c_{i,j}\) is \(X_{k,k+1}\), in the one mesh variable straddling leg \(k\). ∎

Lemma 7.14 (ear lemma). Let a polygon \(\mathsf P\) have legs, cyclically, \(s,U,R,V\): \(s\) one leg, \(U,V\) runs of even lengths \(2d_1,2d_2\) with \(d_1+d_2\ge1\), \(R\) a run of odd length whose end legs have the class (position parity) \(\pi+1\), where \(\pi\) is the class of \(s\). Let \(\mu^\pm\) be the legs of \(U\cup V\) of class \(\pi+1\), resp. \(\pi\). Members are chords separating \(s\) from \(R\) and cutting \(U,V\) into even runs; \(A_Q\) is the side containing \(R\); the rectangle \(\rect{Q}\) is (class-\(\pi\) legs of \(A_Q\)) × (class-\((\pi+1)\) legs of the other side); \(\mathsf P_Q\) is the polygon between \(s\) and \(Q\). Let \(K\) be a set of members (containing the chord cutting off \(R\) if \(|R|\ge3\)), and \(Z'\) the massless locus cut out by: (z1) the \(\mu^+\) legs are pairwise orthogonal; (z2) each \(\mu^+\) leg is orthogonal to each leg of \(R\); (z3) the mesh variables of \(\rect{Q}\) vanish for \(Q\in K\). Assume (gen): no odd diagonal of \(\mathsf P\) vanishes identically on \(Z'\). Then \(N(\mathsf P)\), \(N(\mathsf P_Q)\), \(N^{\neg K}(\mathsf P_Q)\) (\(Q\in K\)) and \(N^{\neg K}(\mathsf P)\) all vanish on \(Z'\).

Summary of the proof (full proof: Supplement S1, Lemma S1.2.6)

The class-\(\pi\) legs of \(U\cup V\) together with \(s\) form a same-class set \(G\), and (z1)–(z2) say exactly that \(Z'\) lies in the \(G\)-separated locus of \(\mathsf P\); so \(N(\mathsf P)\equiv0\) by Theorem 2.9. The same holds for each \(\mathsf P_Q\), whose new side \(\hat Q\) carries the class-\((\pi+1)\) momentum of \(A_Q\): the mesh variables pairing a \(\mu^+\) leg with \(\hat Q\) are sums over \(A_Q\) that vanish by (z1)–(z3), and the massless form changes only mesh variables that play no role (Lemma 7.13). Grouping by the members (Lemma 7.7(b)) and a downward induction on \(\succ\), using that no member vanishes identically on \(Z'\) (witness: the \(\mu^+\) momenta set to zero, the rest generic), then give \(N^{\neg K}(\mathsf P_Q)\equiv0\) and \(N^{\neg K}(\mathsf P)\equiv0\).

The rectangle conditions (z3) are needed: without them the conclusion fails on every instance tested (Supplement S4).

Lemma 7.15 (only the extreme term survives). Under (H1)–(H4): \(b_Q|_H=0\) for \(Q\ne b\), \(a_Q|_H=0\) for \(Q\ne t\), and every \(\varphi(Q,Q')\) is regular at generic points of \(H\).

Summary of the proof (full proof: Supplement S1, Lemma S1.2.7)

For \(Q\succ b\), apply the ear lemma to the A-side polygon of \(Q\), with \(s=\hat Q\), \(R=A_b\), and the members strictly between \(Q\) and \(b\), together with \(b\), as \(K\): then \(N^{\neg K}\) of that polygon is \(b_Q\), and Lemma 7.12 puts the image of \(H\) inside the locus \(Z'\) (the rectangle conditions hold because the rectangles of members lie in \(S\)). So \(b_Q\) vanishes on \(H\) (RF5). The statement for \(a_Q\), \(Q\prec t\), is the mirror. For the regularity of \(\varphi\), the chain expansion of \(N_{\Omega(Q,Q')}\) has factors \(N^{\neg K}\) of strip regions, each vanishing on \(H\) by the ear lemma with a single-leg \(R\), hence each divisible by \(x\) (RF1).

Hypothesis (H3) is needed: for a proportionality class with no \(\cP_1\) member the conclusion fails in a tested instance (Supplement S4).

7.5 The value of the strip factor

It remains to show \(\varphi(t,b)|_H=(-1)^d\). This is a statement about one alternating polygon on a universal locus, proved by induction on its size and then pulled back to \(L_S\).

Universal loci. Let \(\mathsf P\) have legs \(s,u_1,\dots,u_{2d_1},r,v_1,\dots,v_{2d_2}\) (\(n=2d+2\), \(d=d_1+d_2\)), with class \(\pi=\{s,u_{\mathrm{even}},v_{\mathrm{odd}}\}\) and class \(\pi+1=\{r,u_{\mathrm{odd}},v_{\mathrm{even}}\}\). Let \(c_\star\) be the mesh variable straddling \(s\) (the pair \(v_{2d_2},u_1\), class \(\pi+1\)) and \(c_\star'\) the one straddling \(r\) (the pair \(u_{2d_1},v_1\), class \(\pi\)). Members are the chords \(Q=(i,j)\in[0,d_1]\times[0,d_2]\setminus\{(d_1,0),(0,d_2)\}\) with \(A_Q=u_{2i+1..2d_1}\cup\{r\}\cup v_{1..2j}\); \(Q\preceq Q'\) iff \(A_Q\subseteq A_{Q'}\), with meet and join taken coordinatewise; \(\hat I:=I\cup\{Q_\top,Q_\bot\}\), where \(Q_\top:=(0,d_2)\) and \(Q_\bot:=(d_1,0)\) are the two non-chord labels, and a set \(I\) of members is closed if \(\hat I\) is closed under meet and join. For a set \(I\) of members, \(\Lambda^I\) is the subspace of the massless kinematic space of \(\mathsf P\) on which every same-class mesh variable vanishes except \(c_\star=c_\star'=:x\), and the mesh variables of \(\rect{Q}\) vanish for \(Q\in I\); \(\Lambda:=\Lambda^\emptyset\), and a subscript 0 means \(x=0\). In the Gram model, massless kinematics of \(\mathsf P\) is identified with symmetric \(n\times n\) matrices \(\mathsf G\) with zero diagonal and zero row sums (\(X_A=\sum_{a\ne b\in A}\mathsf G_{ab}\), mesh variable \(=-2\mathsf G_{ab}\)); write \(\mathsf G_{\rm mix}\) for its block with rows of class \(\pi+1\) and columns of class \(\pi\). The image of the mesh map is the kernel of the one relation, and \(\Lambda_0\) is the space of balanced mixed blocks \(\mathsf G_{\rm mix}\) (all row and column sums 0), of dimension \((k-1)^2\), \(k=n/2\).

Lemma 7.16 (genericity and regions).

  1. No odd diagonal of \(\mathsf P\) vanishes identically on \(\Lambda_0\); for distinct odd diagonals \(Q,Q'\), \(X_{Q'}\notin\mathrm{span}(X_Q,x)\) on \(\Lambda\).
  2. If \(d_1\ge1\), \(b'\) is the chord cutting off \((u_{2d_1-1},u_{2d_1},r)\) and \(\Lambda'':=\Lambda^{\{b'\}}\), every odd diagonal other than \(b'\) is non-zero on \(\Lambda''_0\).
  3. If \(\Xi\) is cut out by “every same-class mesh variable is 0 except \(c_\star,c_\star',c_\circ\)” (\(c_\circ\) a class-\((\pi+1)\) mesh variable \(\ne c_\star\)) and some mixed mesh variables, then \(c_\star,c_\star'\) are linearly independent on \(\Xi\).
  4. If \(I\) is closed, no non-member odd diagonal vanishes identically on \(\Lambda^I_0\).
  5. For \(q\in I\), let \(c_\circ\) be the mesh variable of the two end legs of \(A_q\) and \(\Xi_q\) the space where the same-class mesh variables vanish except \(c_\star,c_\star',c_\circ\), and those of \(\rect{q}\) vanish. On \(\Xi_q\), for every member \(Q\), \(X_Q=c_\star'-[c_\circ\subseteq A_Q]\,c_\circ-2\sum_{\rect{Q}}\mathsf G\), and the forms \(c_\star\), \(c_\star'\), \(X_Q\) (\(Q\in I\setminus q\)) are non-zero and pairwise non-proportional.
  6. (Regions.) On \(\Lambda^I\), \(X_Q=x\) for every \(Q\in I\), and for \(y\succ z\) in \(\hat I\) the region \(\Omega(y,z)\) lies on its own locus \(\Lambda^J\), \(J:=I\cap(z,y)\): its same-class mesh variables vanish, its two sides have mass \(x\), and its rectangles are contained in those of \(\mathsf P\).

Summary of the proof (full proof, including the case analysis of (d): Supplement S1, Lemma S1.2.9)

(a) On \(\Lambda_0\), \(X_A\) is \(2\langle \mathsf G_{\rm mix},(\mathbb{P}1_{R_A})\otimes(\mathbb{P}1_{C_A})\rangle\), with \(R_A,C_A\) the class-\((\pi+1)\) and class-\(\pi\) legs of the side \(A\) and \(\mathbb P\) the centring projector; the pairing is non-degenerate on balanced matrices, which gives non-vanishing and, comparing the four combinations of complements, non-proportionality. (b) is the same argument on the block with one column removed. (c) On the image of the mesh map the one relation reads \(c_\star+c_\circ-c_\star'=0\). (d) Each odd diagonal is detected by a balanced matrix \((e_{w_1}-e_{w_2})(e_{c_1}-e_{c_2})^{\mathsf T}\) avoiding the rectangles of \(I\); a case analysis on the position of the diagonal shows that if every such choice were blocked, meets and joins of the blocking members would produce a member of \(\hat I\) outside \(I\), against closedness. (e), (f) are column-sum bookkeeping in the Gram model.

Closedness in (d) is needed: the statement fails for every non-closed \(I\) tested (Supplement S4).

Theorem 7.17 (value on the universal locus). For every even \(n\ge4\), every choice of \(d_1,d_2\) and every closed \(I\): \(N(\mathsf P)=(-1)^dx+x^2R\) on \(\Lambda^I\), with \(R\) regular wherever the non-member odd diagonals are non-zero. Consequently, under (H1)–(H4), \(\varphi(t,b)|_H=(-1)^d\).

Summary of the proof (full proof: Supplement S1, Theorem S1.2.10)

Step 1 (constancy). \(\Lambda_0\) lies in the \(T\)-separated locus of a whole class, so \(N\equiv0\) there (Theorem 2.9) and \(\varphi:=N/x\) is regular at generic points of \(\Lambda_0\), homogeneous of degree 0. Near a hyperplane \(X_Q=0\) of \(\Lambda_0\), write \(N=N^{\neg Q}+N_LN_R/X_Q\) (Lemma 7.7). If \(Q\) separates \(s\) from \(r\), both sides are on \(T\)-separated loci at \(x=X_Q=0\) (the straddling mesh variables are handled by Lemma 7.13), so \(N_L,N_R\) lie in the ideal \((x,X_Q)\); expanding \(x\varphi\) in this ideal and using that \(N\) itself vanishes at \(x=0\) shows that the \(X_Q\)-pole cancels. If \(Q\) does not separate \(s\) from \(r\), one side is on its locus up to a mesh variable shifted by \(X_Q\), and the term is regular. So \(\varphi|_{\Lambda_0}\) has no poles and is a constant (RF3, RF4). Step 2 (\(I=\emptyset\)). The constant is \((-1)^{(n-2)/2}\), by induction on \(n\): cut off the last three legs by the chord \(b'\); on \(\Lambda''\) the four-point piece equals \(-X_{b'}\), the remaining polygon is on its own smaller universal locus, and the \(b'\)-free part is \(O(x^2)\) because it vanishes on the two distinct hyperplanes \(c_\star=0\) and \(c_\star'=0\) (the ear lemma for two decompositions). Step 3 (key lemma). For closed \(I\) and \(q\in I\), \(N^{\neg q}(\mathsf P)=O(x^2)\) on \(\Lambda^I\), by the same two-hyperplane argument on \(\Xi_q\) together with the absence of poles along \(X_Q=0\), \(Q\in I\setminus q\) (one of the two regions cut by \(Q\) avoids \(q\) and lies on its locus). Step 4 (closed \(I\)). Induction on \(|I|\): grouping by one member \(q\) splits \(\mathsf P\) into two regions on their own universal loci (Lemma 7.16(f)), whose leading terms multiply to \((-1)^d x^2\), divided by \(X_q=x\). Step 5 (pull-back). The region \(\Omega(t,b)\) of the parent maps \(L_S\) into its universal locus with \(I=K\cap(b,t)\) (Lemma 7.12); \(I\) is closed because the rectangles of the meet and join of two crossing members lie in the union of theirs, so both are in \(K\) by (H2).

Proof of Theorem 7.9. By Lemma 7.11, \(x\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) is a sum of terms regular at generic points of \(H\); by Lemma 7.15 every term except \((t,b)\) restricts to 0 on \(H\); by Theorem 7.17, \(\varphi(t,b)|_H=(-1)^d\); and \(a_t,b_b\) are the full child amplitudes. ∎

The theorem holds for every class \(K\subseteq\cP_1\) with a top and a bottom satisfying (H1)–(H4), not only for \(K^*\).

7.6 The children

Let \(P\) be an odd chord with dual points \(\alpha\) (odd) and \(\omega+1\), so \(A=A_P=[\alpha,\omega]\), \(B=B_P=[\omega+1,\alpha-1]\). For \(L\in\{A,B\}\), with \(L^c\) the other side, the child on side \(L\) is the polygon with legs \(L\) (in order) and one new leg \(\hat P\) in place of \(L^c\), carrying the total momentum of \(L^c\); it has \(m:=|L|+1\) legs, labelled so as to preserve parity (for the A-child, child dual point \(j\mapsto\alpha+j-1\) and \(\hat P\) is the even child leg \(m\); for the B-child, \(\hat P\) is the odd child leg 1). Let \(\rho_L\) be the linear map from the parent to the child kinematic space that sends each child planar variable to the parent \(X\) of the corresponding chord. The pair map \(\pi_L\) sends a pair inside \(L\) to itself, a pair \(\{u,w\}\) with \(u\in\intr L\), \(w\in L^c\) to \(\{u,\hat P\}\), and every other pair to nothing. The child missing set is \(M_L:=\pi_L(M)\) and the child set \(S_L\) its complement: \(S_L=\{t\subseteq L:t\in S\}\cup\{\{u,\hat P\}: u\in\intr L,\ \{u,w\}\in S\ \text{for all}\ w\in L^c\}\).

Proposition 7.18 (children). Let \(P\in\cP_1\).

  1. \(\rho_L(L_S\cap\{X_P=0\})=L^{(m)}_{S_L}\), the coordinate locus of \(S_L\) at size \(m\).
  2. \(m\) is even and \(4\le m\le N-2\).
  3. If \(S\in\Fc_N\), then \(S_L\in\Fc_m\) if and only if \(L\) is linked.
  4. If \(K\subseteq\cP_1\) is a flank class with top \(t\) and bottom \(b\) and \(H:=L_S\cap\{X_t=0\}\), then \(H=L_S\cap\{X_b=0\}\), and \(\rho_{B_t}\), \(\rho_{A_b}\) map \(H\) onto the child loci of \(S_{B_t}\), \(S_{A_b}\). If \(\NLSM_m\not\equiv0\) on a child locus, then \(\NLSM_m\circ\rho_L\not\equiv0\) on \(H\).

Summary of the proof (full proof: Supplement S1, Proposition S1.3.1)

The child mesh variables pull back to parent ones: a child pair inside \(L\) other than the two end legs of \(L\) gives the same parent \(c\); the end-leg pair gives \(c+X_P\); and \(\{u,\hat P\}\) gives the block sum \(\sum_{w\in L^c}c_{u,w}\). This gives “\(\subseteq\)” in (1). For “\(\supseteq\)”, a point of the child locus is lifted pair by pair to the missing coordinates of the parent, using one chosen pair of \(\ein(P)\) (for \(L=A\)) or \(\oin(P)\) (for \(L=B\)) to satisfy the parent's one relation; this is where \(P\in\cP_1\) is needed, and the statement fails off \(\cP_1\). (2) is \(3\le|L|\le N-3\). For (3), a member of \(\cG_m\) whose cut set avoids \(\hat P\) pulls back to a member of \(\cG_N\), which \(M\) meets because \(S\in\Fc_N\); one whose cut set contains \(\hat P\) pulls back to the separated set \(\sep_L(\Theta)\) of an admissible separator \(\Theta=T\setminus\{\hat P\}\) of \(L\), and every admissible separator arises so. (4) follows from the chord formula (\(h_t=h_b\)) and (1).

Interpretation. So the lower-point amplitudes that appear in the residue are the physical factorisation channels of the parent, restricted to coordinate loci that are again of the kind the theorem is about; the linkedness of \(B_t\) and \(A_b\) is exactly what keeps them free of listed zeros.

7.7 Proof of clause 1

Proof of Theorem 3.1, clause 1. By strong induction on even \(N\ge4\) we show \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\) for every \(S\in\Fc_N\); the base \(N=4\) is in §4.4. Let \(N\ge6\) and \(S\in\Fc_N\); \(S\) is non-degenerate (Proposition 4.4). If \(\cP_1=\emptyset\), Theorem 6.8(b) applies. Otherwise let \(P^*\) be an innermost chord (Corollary 7.3) and \(K^*\) its flank class. By Proposition 8.15 (which rests on the combinatorial core of §8), (Bot) holds, so by Proposition 7.5 \(K^*\) has top \(t=P^*\) and a bottom \(b\) with \(A_b\) linked. By Lemma 7.6, \(K^*\) is the full proportionality class of \(X_{P^*}\), every member at \(+X_{P^*}\), and \(K^*\subseteq\cP_1\). So (H1)–(H4) hold and Theorem 7.9 gives \((X_{P^*}\NLSM_N|_{L_S})|_H=\pm a_{P^*}|_H\,b_b|_H\). The B-child of \(P^*\) is a candidate set because \(B^*\) is linked (\(P^*\in\cS_B\)), and the A-child of \(b\) because \(A_b\) is linked (Proposition 7.18(3)); their sizes are even and at most \(N-2\). By the induction hypothesis and Proposition 7.18(4), \(a_{P^*}|_H\) and \(b_b|_H\) are non-zero rational functions on the linear space \(H\); rational functions on a linear space form an integral domain, so their product is non-zero. Hence \(\NLSM_N|_{L_S}\) has a genuine simple pole along \(H\), and \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\). ∎

8. The combinatorial core

Everything in this section is a statement about the missing set \(M\) and arcs of the leg circle \(C_N\); no amplitude appears. We prove that (Bot) holds at an innermost chord when \(S\in\Fc_N\). The heart is §8.2, a self-contained lemma about three families of separated pairs on a circle, (Tri); a reader interested only in the combinatorics can read §8.2 on its own. §8.1 explains why (Tri) is the right statement, and §8.3–§8.4 derive (Bot) from it.

8.1 From the chord picture to three separators

Definition 8.0 (runs of an even separator). Let \(P'\in\cS_B\) with sides \(A'\), \(B'\), and let \(\Theta\) be an \(M\)-free even separator of \(A'\) (an \(M\)-free admissible separator all of whose legs are even).

  • An occupied run is a run of \(A'\setminus\Theta\) containing a missing oo pair. Every pair of \(\oin(P')\) lies inside a single run, since a missing oo pair joining two runs would lie in \(\sep_{A'}(\Theta)\).
  • \(\Theta\) is spread if it has at least two occupied runs.
  • The run chord \(Q_Z\) of an occupied run \(Z\) is the odd chord with \(A_{Q_Z}=Z\). (An occupied run has odd end legs — ends of \(A'\) or neighbours of even \(\Theta\)-legs — and at least three legs, since it holds a missing pair of non-adjacent legs.)
  • For \(Q\in\cP_0\) with \(A_Q\subsetneq A'\) and an \(M\)-free separator \(\tau\) of \(B_Q\): \(R_\tau\) is the piece of \(B_Q\setminus\tau\) that contains \(\intr B'\) (with \(y\) adjoined when \(\tau=\{y\}\), \(y\) even), and \(R_\tau^c:=C_N\setminus R_\tau\). For two occupied runs \(\RX=[x_0,x_1]\) before \(\RY=[y_0,y_1]\) along \(A'\), an \(M\)-free separator \(\tau\) of \(B_{Q_\RX}\) is non-reaching if \(y_0\notin R_\tau^c\) and reaching otherwise; an \(M\)-free separator \(\sigma\) of \(B_{Q_\RY}\) is non-reaching if \(x_1\notin R_\sigma^c\), reaching otherwise.

With \(P^*\) an innermost chord, \(A^*\) its odd-ended side and \(K^*\) its flank class:

(NoSpread)  \(P^*\) has no spread \(M\)-free even separator of \(A^*\).

The bridge. Suppose \(P^*\) had a spread \(M\)-free even separator \(\Theta\) of \(A^*\), with occupied runs \(\RX\) before \(\RY\). The run chords \(Q_\RX,Q_\RY\) are in \(\cP_1\) (Lemma 8.5) but, since \(P^*\) is innermost, not in \(\cS_B\), so their even-ended sides \(B_\RX=C_N\setminus \RX\) and \(B_\RY=C_N\setminus \RY\) carry \(M\)-free separators \(\tau\) and \(\sigma\). Three facts about \(M\) are then known: no missing pair joins two runs of \(A^*\setminus\Theta\), none joins two runs of \(B_\RX\setminus\tau\), and none joins two runs of \(B_\RY\setminus\sigma\). The three-separator lemma forgets \(M\) and keeps only these three sets of separated pairs; it says that some member \(S_G\) of \(\cG_N\) lies inside their union. Then \(M\) misses \(S_G\), against \(S\in\Fc_N\). The exact passage, including why \(\tau,\sigma\) can be taken reaching and of the right shape, is Lemma 8.9.

8.2 The three-separator lemma

Setting. \(N\) is even and indices are cyclic mod \(N\). The odd-ended side is \(A'=[1,\omega]\) with \(\omega\) odd; the even-ended side is \(W=[\omega+1,N]\), with \(|W|\ge3\), so \(\intr W=[\omega+2,N-1]\ne\emptyset\). \(\Theta\) is a non-empty set of even legs in \(\intr A'\). \(\RX=[x_0,x_1]\) and \(\RY=[y_0,y_1]\) are two runs of \(A'\setminus\Theta\), \(\RX\) before \(\RY\), each with at least three legs; \(\gamma:=x_1+1\) and \(\gamma':=y_0-1\) are in \(\Theta\). \(B_\RX:=C_N\setminus \RX\), \(B_\RY:=C_N\setminus \RY\).

  • \(\cT\) is the set of leg sets \(G\) with \(|G|\ge2\), all of one parity.
  • A separator \(\tau\) of \(B_\RX\) is admissible if it is a single leg of \(\intr B_\RX\) or a set of at least two odd legs of \(\intr B_\RX\); likewise \(\sigma\) for \(B_\RY\). (Adm): an admissible separator containing an even leg is an even singleton. “\(\tau\) odd” means all its legs are odd.
  • W-avoiding: \(\tau\cap\intr W=\emptyset\), and if \(\tau\) is odd, \(\tau\cap W=\emptyset\); the same for \(\sigma\).
  • For \(D\in\{\tau,\sigma\}\) let \(\RX_D\) be its run (\(\RX_\tau=\RX\), \(\RX_\sigma=\RY\)). A \(D\)-piece is a component of \(C_N\setminus(\RX_D\cup D)\); \(\intr W\) lies in one \(D\)-piece, \(R_D\).
  • Reaching: \(y_0\notin R_\tau\) and \(x_1\notin R_\sigma\).
  • Separated-pair sets: \(\cC_A:=\sep_{A'}(\Theta)\); \(\cC_\RX\): the pairs \(\{u,v\}\), \(u,v\notin \RX\cup\tau\), in different \(\tau\)-pieces (that is, \(\sep_{B_\RX}(\tau)\)); \(\cC_\RY\) likewise with \(\RY,\sigma\). \(\cC:=\cC_A\cup\cC_\RX\cup\cC_\RY\).

The statement (Tri) (proved as Theorem 8.4). For every such configuration and all admissible, W-avoiding, reaching separators \(\tau\) of \(B_\RX\) and \(\sigma\) of \(B_\RY\), some \(G\in\cT\) has \(S_G\subseteq \cC\).

Facts. (i) \(\tau\) is reaching iff \(\tau\cap[y_0,\omega+1]\ne\emptyset\); if \(\tau\) is odd, \(\tau\cap[y_0,\omega]\ne\emptyset\). Mirror: \(\sigma\cap(\{N\}\cup[1,x_1])\ne\emptyset\); if \(\sigma\) is odd, \(\sigma\cap[1,x_1]\ne\emptyset\). (Read \(B_\RX\) as the arc \(\gamma\to\dots\to\omega+1\to\intr W\to\dots\to x_0-1\); \(y_0\) and \(\intr W\) lie in different \(\tau\)-pieces iff \(\tau\) meets \([y_0,\omega+1]\); an odd \(\tau\) misses \(W\) by W-avoidance.) (ii) Odd legs are never in \(\Theta\), so the legs of an odd \(\tau\) lie in \(A'\setminus(\Theta\cup \RX)\), and those of an odd \(\sigma\) in \(A'\setminus(\Theta\cup \RY)\).

Reach sets (for odd separators). \(Z_\RX^R=\emptyset\) if \(x_1\in\sigma\); otherwise walk clockwise from \(\gamma\) over legs not in \(\sigma\cup \RY\), let \(f_R\) be the last leg of \(\Theta\cup W\) on the walk, and \(Z_\RX^R:=[\gamma,f_R]\). \(Z_\RX^L\) is the mirror: counterclockwise from \(x_0-1\) over legs not in \(\sigma\cup \RY\), up to the last leg of \(\Theta\cup W\); \(\emptyset\) if \(x_0\in\sigma\). \(Z_\RY^L\), \(Z_\RY^R\) are defined from \(\gamma'\) (counterclockwise) and \(y_1+1\) (clockwise) over legs not in \(\tau\cup \RX\); \(\emptyset\) if \(y_0\in\tau\), respectively \(y_1\in\tau\). Put \(Z_\RX:=Z_\RX^L\cup Z_\RX^R\), \(Z_\RY:=Z_\RY^L\cup Z_\RY^R\), and the reach witness \(G':=(\tau\setminus Z_\RX)\cup(\sigma\setminus Z_\RY)\). In words: for each end \(e\) of \(\RX\) with \(e\notin\sigma\), \(Z_\RX\) contains the legs from \(e\)'s outer neighbour up to the last \(\Theta\cup W\) leg met by walking away from \(\RX\) over legs not in \(\sigma\cup \RY\); an end of \(\RX\) that lies in \(\sigma\) contributes nothing. (We call this the end-blocked reading.)

The reading matters for the proof below, not for the result: if the walk may start at an end of \(\RX\) that lies in \(\sigma\), fact (Z1) fails (Supplement S4), while the reach lemma still holds.

Facts (Z0)–(Z3).

  • (Z0) \(Z_\RX^R,Z_\RY^L\subseteq[\gamma,\gamma']\) and \(Z_\RX^L,Z_\RY^R\subseteq[y_1+1,x_0-1]\) (cyclically).
  • (Z1) \(Z_\RX\cap(\sigma\cup \RX\cup \RY)=\emptyset\), and the arc from the relevant end of \(\RX\) through \(Z_\RX^\bullet\) is \(\sigma\)-free.
  • (Z2) If an end \(e\) of \(\RX\) is not in \(\sigma\) and \(v\in\Theta\cup W\) is reached from \(e\) by a \(\sigma\)-free arc avoiding \(\RY\), then \(v\in Z_\RX\).
  • (Z3) If \(u\in Z_\RX\setminus\Theta\) is a leg of \(A'\), its whole run of \(A'\setminus\Theta\) lies in \(Z_\RX\).

Mirror statements hold for \(Z_\RY\).

Proof. (Z0) The walk of \(Z_\RX^R\) starts at \(\gamma\) and goes clockwise over legs not in \(\RY\), so it stops at \(y_0\) at the latest; that of \(Z_\RX^L\) stops at \(y_1\) at the latest; the same for \(Z_\RY\). (Z1) Each \(Z_\RX^\bullet\) is an initial segment of its walk, which avoids \(\sigma\cup \RY\) by construction and \(\RX\) by (Z0); if \(Z_\RX^\bullet\) at an end \(e\) is non-empty, then \(e\notin\sigma\), so \(e\) together with \(Z_\RX^\bullet\) is \(\sigma\)-free. (Z2) The walk from \(e\)'s outer neighbour runs at least through the whole \(\sigma\cup \RY\)-free arc up to \(v\), so the last \(\Theta\cup W\) leg is at or beyond \(v\). (If the arc runs through \(\RX\) and leaves at the other end \(e'\), apply this at \(e'\notin\sigma\).) (Z3) \(Z_\RX^\bullet=[\mathrm{start},f]\) is an arc with both endpoints in \(\Theta\cup W\); the run of \(u\) contains no \(\Theta\cup W\) leg, meets \([\mathrm{start},f]\) at \(u\) and contains neither endpoint, so it lies inside. (If start \(=f\), (Z3) is vacuous.) ∎

For a \(D\)-piece \(V\ne R_D\) put \(G_V:=(\Theta\cap V)\cup\{b\in D: b\ \text{even and adjacent to a leg of}\ V\}\). If \(D\) is odd, \(G_V=\Theta\cap V\).

Lemma 8.1 (closed pieces). Let \(D\in\{\tau,\sigma\}\) be admissible and \(V\ne R_D\) a \(D\)-piece with \(|G_V|\ge2\). Then \(G_V\in\cT\) and \(S_{G_V}\subseteq\cC_A\cup\cC_D\).

Proof (for \(D=\tau\); for \(\sigma\) read \(\RY,\sigma,\cC_\RY\); “reaching” is not used). (0) \(V\subseteq A'\setminus \RX\). \(V\cap \RX=\emptyset\) by definition. If \(V\) held a leg of \(W\) it would hold \(\omega+1\), \(N\) or a leg of \(\intr W\); as \(\omega+1\) is adjacent to \(\omega+2\in\intr W\) and \(N\) to \(N-1\in\intr W\), neither in \(\RX\cup\tau\), in each case \(V=R_\tau\), excluded. \(V\) is an arc; let \(b^-,b^+\) be the legs just outside its two ends (\(b^-\ne b^+\), as \(|\RX|\ge3\)), both in \(\RX\cup\tau\), and \(\bar V=\{b^-\}\cup V\cup\{b^+\}\). The legs of \(G_V\) are even (\(\Theta\) is even), and two even legs are never adjacent, so \(|G_V|\ge2\) gives \(G_V\in\cT\); \(G_V\subseteq\bar V\). Legs of \(G_V\) outside \(\Theta\) are even \(\tau\)-legs, so by (Adm) they exist only if \(\tau=\{t\}\) is an even singleton; then \(t\in\{b^-,b^+\}\), because both \(\tau\)-pieces of a singleton are adjacent to \(t\) (\(t\) lies in the interior of \(B_\RX\), so \(t\notin\{x_0-1,x_1+1\}\)), and \(t\in G_V\).

(1) Components. List \(G_V=g_1,\dots,g_k\) in the order of \(\bar V\). The components of \(C_N\setminus G_V\) are the open arcs \(J_i=(g_i,g_{i+1})\), \(i\lt k\), inside \(V\), and one outer arc \(J_0\). A pair separated by \(G_V\) has one leg \(u\) in some \(J_i\), and the other, \(v\notin J_i\cup G_V\). \(J_i\) avoids \(W\) and \(\Theta\), so it lies in one run \(R_i\ne \RX\) of \(A'\setminus\Theta\); also \(u\notin \RX\cup\tau\). (a) \(v\in A'\setminus\Theta\), \(v\notin R_i\): different runs, \(\{u,v\}\in\cC_A\). (b) \(v\in R_i\setminus J_i\): \(R_i\) contains the end \(g\in\{g_i,g_{i+1}\}\) between \(u\) and \(v\); \(g\notin\Theta\), so \(\tau=\{g\}\) with \(g\in\{b^-,b^+\}\). Then \(v\notin V\) (otherwise the \(u\)–\(v\) routes inside \(V\) and inside \(R_i\) would cover \(C_N\), so \(V\cup R_i\supseteq W\), against \(V,R_i\subseteq A'\)); \(v\notin \RX\), \(v\ne g\); so \(u\in V\) and \(v\notin V\) lie in different \(\tau\)-pieces: \(\{u,v\}\in\cC_\RX\). (c) \(v\in\Theta\cup W\): \(v\notin V\), \(v\notin \RX\); if \(v\in\tau\), then \(\tau=\{v\}\) is an even singleton (an odd \(\tau\) misses \(W\) by W-avoidance), so \(v\in G_V\), excluded; so \(\{u,v\}\in\cC_\RX\). Since \(C_N=(A'\setminus\Theta)\cup\Theta\cup W\), these cases cover every \(v\). ∎

Lemma 8.2. Let \(\tau,\sigma\) be admissible, W-avoiding and reaching. If no \(\tau\)-piece \(V\ne R_\tau\) and no \(\sigma\)-piece \(V\ne R_\sigma\) has \(|G_V|\ge2\), then \(\tau\) and \(\sigma\) are odd and \(|G'|\ge2\).

Proof. (A) Both are odd. If \(\tau=\{e\}\) with \(e\) even, then \(e\in[y_0,\omega+1]\) by (i); the \(\tau\)-pieces are \(V_1=[\gamma,e-1]\) and \(V_2=[e+1,x_0-1]\supseteq\intr W\), so \(V_1\ne R_\tau\), and \(G_{V_1}\supseteq\{\gamma,e\}\), excluded. If \(\sigma=\{g\}\) with \(g\) even, \(g\in\{N\}\cup[1,x_1]\); the \(\sigma\)-piece of \(\gamma'\) is \(V'=(g,\gamma']\), which misses \(\intr W\), and \(G_{V'}\supseteq\{g,\gamma'\}\), excluded. By (Adm) both are odd, so the hypothesis reads: every \(D\)-piece \(V\ne R_D\) has \(|\Theta\cap V|\le1\).

(B) \(|G'|\ge2\). Let \(e\) be the first \(\tau\)-leg clockwise from \(\gamma\) (\(e\le\omega\) by (i)) and \(f\) the first \(\sigma\)-leg counterclockwise from \(\gamma'\) (\(f\ge1\)); let \(R_\gamma\) be the run of \(A'\setminus\Theta\) starting at \(\gamma+1\) and \(R^{\gamma'}\) the one ending at \(\gamma'-1\). (1) \(V_1=[\gamma,e-1]\) is the \(\tau\)-piece of \(\gamma\) and misses \(\intr W\), so \(\Theta\cap[\gamma,e-1]=\{\gamma\}\) and \(e\in R_\gamma\subseteq[\gamma+1,y_1]\); mirror: \(f\in R^{\gamma'}\subseteq[x_0,\gamma'-1]\). (2) \(e\notin Z_\RX\): \(e\notin Z_\RX^L\) by (Z0). If \(e\in Z_\RX^R\), then \(x_1\notin\sigma\) and the walk from \(\gamma\) reaches a \(\Theta\cup W\) leg \(f_R\ge e\); so \(R_\gamma=[\gamma+1,r_1]\ne \RY\), the arc \([\gamma,r_1+1]\) is free of \(\sigma\cup \RY\), and \(r_1+1\in\Theta\). So the \(\sigma\)-piece of \(\gamma\) contains \(\gamma\) and \(r_1+1\), both in \(\Theta\); it is not \(R_\sigma\) (it lies in \([a+1,\gamma']\) for a \(\sigma\)-leg \(a\in[1,x_1]\), missing \(\intr W\)); excluded. (3) \(f\notin Z_\RY\) by the mirror argument. (4) So \(e,f\in G'\). If \(e=f\), then \(R_\gamma=R^{\gamma'}\), so \(\gamma\ne\gamma'\). Let \(a\) be the last \(\sigma\)-leg in \([1,x_1]\) and \(b\) the first \(\tau\)-leg in \([y_0,\omega]\); \(a\le x_1\lt e\lt y_0\le b\). By (Z0), \(a\notin Z_\RY^L\) and \(b\notin Z_\RX^R\). If \(a\in Z_\RY^R\), the walk from \(y_1+1\) reached \(a\), so \(a\in[1,x_0-1]\); if \(b\in Z_\RX^L\), the arc \([b,x_0-1]\) is \(\sigma\)-free, so \(\sigma\cap[1,x_0-1]=\emptyset\). Both cannot hold, so \(a\in G'\) or \(b\in G'\), and either differs from \(e\). (For singleton separators \(e=f\) cannot occur: a singleton \(\sigma=\{a\}\) has \(a\le x_1\lt e\), and a singleton \(\tau=\{b\}\) has \(b\ge y_0\gt f\).) ∎

Lemma 8.3 (reach lemma). Let \(\tau,\sigma\) be odd, W-avoiding and reaching, and suppose \(|G'|\ge2\). Then \(G'\in\cT\) and \(S_{G'}\subseteq \cC\).

Proof. \(G'\subseteq\tau\cup\sigma\) consists of odd legs, so \(G'\in\cT\). Let \(\{u,v\}\) be a pair with \(u,v\notin G'\) in no separated-pair set; we show that some arc between \(u\) and \(v\) avoids \(G'\).

(1) \(u,v\notin \RX\cup \RY\). Since \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_\RX\), \(u\) or \(v\) is in \(\tau\), or one \(\tau\)-piece holds both; likewise with \(\sigma\). (1a) One \(\tau\)-piece \(V\) and one \(\sigma\)-piece \(V'\) hold both. Let \(I_\tau\subseteq V\), \(I_\sigma\subseteq V'\) be the arcs from \(u\) to \(v\) inside them. If \(I_\tau=I_\sigma\), it avoids \(\tau\cup\sigma\supseteq G'\). Otherwise they are complementary; \(I_\tau\) avoids \(\RX\), so \(\RX\subseteq I_\sigma\), and \(\RY\subseteq I_\tau\); so \(V\ni y_0\) and \(V'\ni x_1\), and, both being reaching, neither \(V\) nor \(V'\) contains \(\intr W\); but \(V\cup V'=C_N\). Contradiction. (1b) \(u\in\tau\) (so \(u\in Z_\RX\), \(u\notin\sigma\) by (Z1), and \(u\) is an odd leg of \(A'\setminus\Theta\) by (ii)). Since \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_\RY\), \(v\in\sigma\) or \(v\) lies in the \(\sigma\)-piece \(J\) of \(u\). If \(v\in\sigma\), then \(v\in Z_\RY\) is an odd leg of \(A'\setminus\Theta\); \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_A\) puts them in one run \(R\), and by (Z3) \(R\subseteq Z_\RX\cap Z_\RY\), which contains no leg of \(G'\). If \(v\in J\): \(J\) contains the \(\sigma\)-free arc from \(u\) to the end \(e\) of \(\RX\) defining \(u\)'s reach set, so \(e\in J\), \(e\notin\sigma\). As \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_A\), \(v\in\Theta\cup W\) or \(v\) is in \(u\)'s run; in the second case (Z3) gives an arc inside the run; in the first, the arc from \(e\) to \(v\) inside \(J\) is \(\sigma\)-free and avoids \(\RY\), so \(v\in Z_\RX\) (Z2), and either the arc between them lies inside \(Z_\RX^\bullet\), or \(J\supseteq \RX\) and the arc \(u\to \RX\to v\) lies in \(Z_\RX^R\cup \RX\cup Z_\RX^L\), with \(\RX\cap G'\subseteq\sigma\cap J=\emptyset\). The cases \(u\in\sigma\), \(v\in\tau\), \(v\in\sigma\) are the same with roles exchanged.

(2) \(u\in \RX\). If \(v\in \RY\): \(\{u,v\}\in\cC_A\). If \(v\in \RX\): \(\sigma\cap \RX\subseteq G'\) (as \(Z_\RY\cap \RX=\emptyset\)), so \(u,v\notin\sigma\), and \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_\RY\) puts them in one \(\sigma\)-piece; the arc inside \(\RX\) between them is \(\sigma\)-free and \(\tau\)-free. If \(v\notin \RX\cup \RY\): if \(v\in\tau\cup\sigma\), it is an odd leg of \(A'\setminus\Theta\) in another run, so \(\{u,v\}\in\cC_A\); otherwise \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_A\) gives \(v\in\Theta\cup W\), and \(\{u,v\}\notin\cC_\RY\) puts \(v\) in the \(\sigma\)-piece \(J\) of \(u\); the arc in \(J\) from \(u\) leaves \(\RX\) at an end \(e\notin\sigma\) and reaches \(v\) \(\sigma\)-free avoiding \(\RY\), so \(v\in Z_\RX\) (Z2), and \(u\to e\to v\) avoids \(G'\). (3) \(u\in \RY\): the mirror of (2). ∎

“Reaching” and W-avoidance are necessary: without W-avoidance the inclusion fails already at \(N=10\), without “reaching” at \(N=14\) (computation, Supplement S4).

Theorem 8.4 (the three-separator lemma). (Tri) holds for every even \(N\). Explicitly: if some \(\tau\)-piece or \(\sigma\)-piece \(V\) other than \(R_\tau,R_\sigma\) has \(|G_V|\ge2\), then \(S_{G_V}\subseteq \cC\) (Lemma 8.1); otherwise \(\tau,\sigma\) are odd and \(|G'|\ge2\) (Lemma 8.2), and \(S_{G'}\subseteq \cC\) (Lemma 8.3).

Remark. The witnesses come from two families, the sets \(G_V\) and the reach witnesses \(G'\), and neither suffices alone (\(N=10\), \(\Theta=\{4\}\), \(\tau=\{5,7\}\), \(\sigma=\{1,3\}\) needs an odd set, Figure 8; \(N=12\), \(\Theta=\{2,6\}\), \(\tau=\{7\}\), \(\sigma=\{12\}\) needs an even set). A single witness family also suffices, at the price of a longer proof: at least two legs of one parity of \(\Theta\cup\tau\cup\sigma\) cut out by one cyclic arc.

8.3 From (Tri) to (NoSpread)

Lemma 8.5 (run chords). Let \(P'\in\cP_1\) with sides \(A',B'\), \(\Theta\) an \(M\)-free even separator of \(A'\) and \(Z\) an occupied run of \(A'\setminus\Theta\). Then \(Q_Z\in\cP_1\). No hypothesis on \(S\).

Proof. \(B_{Q_Z}=B'\cup(A'\setminus Z)\), and every odd leg of \(A'\setminus Z\) lies in a run other than \(Z\) (\(\Theta\) is even). Pairs \(\evens(Z)\times\odds(B')\) lie in \(\rect{P'}\subseteq S\); pairs \(\evens(Z)\times\)(odd legs of other runs) lie in \(\sep_{A'}(\Theta)\), hence in \(S\). So \(Q_Z\in\cP_0\); \(\ein(Q_Z)\supseteq\ein(P')\ne\emptyset\), and \(\oin(Q_Z)\ne\emptyset\) because \(Z\) holds a missing oo pair. ∎

Lemma 8.6 (sub-arcs). For arcs \(L'\subseteq L\) and any leg set \(\Theta\), \(\sep_{L'}(\Theta\cap L')\subseteq\sep_L(\Theta)\) (with \(\sep\) defined by the same formula for arbitrary sets).

Proof. The sub-arc of \(L'\) between two legs of \(L'\) equals the sub-arc of \(L\) between them, because \(L\ne C_N\). ∎

Lemma 8.7 (W-avoidance). Let \(P'\) be an odd chord with sides \(A',B'\) and \(B'\) linked, and let \(Q\) be an odd chord with \(A_Q\subseteq A'\). Then every \(M\)-free admissible separator \(\tau\) of \(B_Q\) avoids \(\intr B'\), and an odd one avoids \(B'\). No hypothesis on \(S\).

Proof. Suppose \(\tau_0:=\tau\cap\intr B'\ne\emptyset\). It is an admissible separator of \(B'\) (a singleton, or odd legs of the interior of an even-ended arc). \(\tau\) contains no end leg of \(B'\): those are even, so not in an odd \(\tau\), and a singleton \(\tau\) lies in \(\intr B'\). So \(\tau\cap B'=\tau_0\), and by Lemma 8.6, \(\sep_{B'}(\tau_0)\subseteq\sep_{B_Q}(\tau)\), which contains no missing pair; so \(\tau_0\) is \(M\)-free, against \(B'\) being linked. If \(\tau\) is odd it also misses the even end legs of \(B'\). ∎

Lemma 8.8 (shortening). Let \(P'\) have \(B'\) linked, \(\Theta\) an \(M\)-free even separator of \(A'\), \(\RX\) before \(\RY\) two occupied runs of \(A'\setminus\Theta\), and \(\tau\) an \(M\)-free separator of \(B_{Q_\RX}\). If \(\tau\) is non-reaching, then \(\Theta':=\Theta\setminus R_\tau^c\) is an \(M\)-free even separator of \(A'\) with \(\Theta'\subsetneq\Theta\) that separates \(\RX\) from \(\RY\); so \(\Theta'\) is spread. The mirror holds for \(Q_\RY\). No hypothesis on \(S\).

Proof. (1) By Lemma 8.7, \(\tau\) avoids \(\intr B'\) (and \(B'\) if odd), so \(R_\tau\supseteq B'\), and \(R_\tau^c=C_N\setminus R_\tau\) is an arc with \(\RX\subsetneq R_\tau^c\subseteq A'\) (it contains \(\tau\), or, for an adjoined even \(y\), the other piece). (2) \(R_\tau^c\) extends past \(\RX\) inside \(A'\), where the neighbours of \(\RX\) are in \(\Theta\) (or \(x_0\) is the first leg of \(A'\)); so \(\Theta'\subsetneq\Theta\). (3) The right end of \(R_\tau^c\) along \(A'\) is an odd leg of \(\tau\), or \(x_1\), or \(y-1\) (adjoined \(y\)): in every case odd. If \(y_0-1\in R_\tau^c\), the right end is at least \(y_0\), against non-reaching; so \(y_0-1\in\Theta'\), and \(\RX,\RY\) lie in different runs of \(A'\setminus\Theta'\). (4) \(\Theta'\) is \(M\)-free: a pair of \(\sep_{A'}(\Theta')\) with both legs outside \(\Theta\) is in \(\sep_{A'}(\Theta)\); otherwise one leg is some \(d\in\Theta\setminus\Theta'=\Theta\cap R_\tau^c\). The arc \(R_\tau^c\) contains no leg of \(\Theta'\), so it lies in one run \(R^\sharp\) of \(A'\setminus\Theta'\); when \(\tau=\{y\}\) with \(y\in A'\setminus\Theta'\), \(y\) is adjacent to \(R_\tau^c\) and also lies in \(R^\sharp\). The other leg \(v\) is not in \(R^\sharp\), so \(v\notin R_\tau^c\), \(v\in R_\tau\) and \(v\ne y\): \(v\) lies in the piece of \(B_{Q_\RX}\setminus\tau\) that holds \(\intr B'\). And \(d\notin\RX\cup\tau\) (\(d\in\Theta\) is not in \(\RX\); \(d\) is even, so it is not in an odd \(\tau\); \(d\ne y\) since \(y\in R_\tau\)), so \(d\in R_\tau^c\) lies in another piece; so the pair is in \(\sep_{B_{Q_\RX}}(\tau)\). ∎

When \(|\Theta|=1\) no \(M\)-free separator is non-reaching, since \(\Theta'\) would be empty.

Lemma 8.9 ((NoSpread) from (Tri)). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\) with \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\), and let \(P^*\) be an innermost chord. Then \(P^*\) has no spread \(M\)-free even separator of \(A^*\).

Proof. Suppose not, and choose a spread \(M\)-free even separator \(\Theta\) of \(A^*\) with \(|\Theta|\) minimal. Let \(\RX\) before \(\RY\) be two occupied runs. By Lemma 8.5, \(Q_\RX,Q_\RY\in\cP_1\); their odd-ended sides are proper sub-arcs of \(A^*\), so, \(P^*\) being innermost, neither is in \(\cS_B\), and each even-ended side has an \(M\)-free separator. If one of them were non-reaching, Lemma 8.8 would give a smaller spread \(M\)-free even separator, against minimality. So \(Q_\RX,Q_\RY\) have reaching \(M\)-free separators \(\tau,\sigma\); they are admissible, and W-avoiding by Lemma 8.7 (\(Q_\RX,Q_\RY\) have odd-ended sides inside \(A^*\), and \(P^*\in\cS_B\)). Rotate by an even number of legs so that \(A^*\) starts at leg 1: \(A'=A^*\), \(W=B^*\). The size hypotheses hold: \(\RX\) and \(\RY\) each contain a missing oo pair of non-adjacent legs, so have at least three legs; \(|B^*|\ge3\); \(\Theta\) is a non-empty set of even legs of \(\intr A^*\). The sets \(\cC_A=\sep_{A^*}(\Theta)\), \(\cC_\RX=\sep_{B_\RX}(\tau)\), \(\cC_\RY=\sep_{B_\RY}(\sigma)\) contain no missing pair. By Theorem 8.4 some \(G\in\cT\) has \(S_G\subseteq \cC\); then \(S_G\in\cG_N\) and \(M\cap S_G=\emptyset\), against \(S\in\Fc_N\). ∎

The case \(|\Theta|=1\) is included. Maximality of \(S\) is not used. Whether the shortening step can be avoided is open (§10).

8.4 From (NoSpread) to (Bot)

For an odd chord \(P'\) and a chord \(Q\) with \(A_Q\subseteq A_{P'}\), call \(Q\) o-clean (with respect to \(P'\)) if \(\oin(Q)=\oin(P')\). The four lemmas below are the inputs of Proposition 8.15. Throughout, “clean” means “not missing”.

Lemma 8.10 (side lemma). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\), \(P'\in\cS_B\) with \(A'=[\alpha,\omega]\), \(B'=[\omega+1,\alpha-1]\), and let \(Q\in\cP_1\) with \(A_Q=[a,a_1]\subseteq A'\) and \(\oin(Q)=\oin(P')\) (\(Q=P'\) allowed). Suppose \(A_Q\) is not linked, witnessed by \(\{x\}\) (that is, \(\{x\}\) is an \(M\)-free separator of \(A_Q\)), \(x\) odd, and the piece \([x,a_1]\) contains a pair of \(\oin(Q)\). Then there is \(Z\in\cS_B\) with \(A_Z\subseteq[x,\omega]\subsetneq A'\). Mirror for the piece \([a,x]\).

Summary of the proof (full proof, including the history fact and the case coverage: Supplement S1, Lemma S1.4.1)

Start from the run chord of the piece \([x,a_1]\), which is in \(\cP_1\). While the current chord \(Q_j\) is not in \(\cS_B\), its even-ended side has an \(M\)-free separator; enlarging the chord to the piece containing \(B'\) gives a chord \(Q_{j+1}\in\cP_1\) with a strictly smaller even-ended side, so the descent ends in \(\cS_B\). The point is an invariant: every \(Q_j\) has odd-ended side \([x,w_j]\subseteq[x,\omega]\). It fails only if the chosen separator meets \([\alpha-1,x-2]\). Two auxiliary facts, (s4) “every pair joining \([a,x-1]\) to \([x+1,w_j]\) is clean” and (s5) “every pair joining \(\odds([\alpha,a-1])\) to \([a,w_j]\) is clean”, hold along the descent; (s4) for two even legs rests on a history fact (s6): an even leg of \(A_{Q_j}\setminus[x,a_1]\) was separated from \([\alpha,x-1]\) at an earlier step. With these, each of five possible shapes of the offending separator produces an explicit member \(S_G\in\cG_N\) that \(M\) misses (an odd-chord diamond \(\{y,x\}\) or \(\{a-1,x\}\), or a same-parity set built from the separator and \(x\), or the even legs of a stretch ending at \(a-1\)), against \(S\in\Fc_N\).

Corollary 8.11 (no odd singleton). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\) and \(P^*\) be innermost. No member \(Q\) of \(K^*\), \(P^*\) included, has an odd singleton \(M\)-free separator of \(A_Q\).

Proof. Let \(Q\in K^*\) fail at \(\{x\}\), \(x\) odd. By Proposition 7.5(a), \(A_Q\subseteq A^*\), and \(\oin(Q)=\oin(P^*)\ne\emptyset\). Every pair of \(\oin(Q)\) lies inside one of the two pieces (a pair across avoiding \(x\) is in \(\sep_{A_Q}(\{x\})\)). Lemma 8.10 with \(P'=P^*\) gives \(Z\in\cS_B\) with \(A_Z\subsetneq A^*\), against innermostness. ∎

Lemma 8.12 (thin runs). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\), \(P'\in\cS_B\), and \(Q\in\cP_0\) o-clean with \(A_Q\subsetneq A'\) (so \(A'\setminus A_Q=B_Q\setminus B'\)). Then every \(M\)-free separator \(\tau\) of \(B_Q\) is odd, \(B'\) lies in one run \(R_\natural\) of \(B_Q\setminus\tau\), and every other run is a single even leg. Corollary: if moreover \(\ein(Q)\ne\emptyset\), the chord \(Q^+\) with even-ended side \(R_\natural\) is in \(\cP_1\), o-clean, with \(h_{Q^+}=h_Q\) and \(A_Q\subsetneq A_{Q^+}\subseteq A'\).

Proof. By Lemma 8.7, \(\tau\subseteq(A'\setminus A_Q)\cup\{\text{end legs of }B'\}\). Case \(\tau=\{y\}\), \(y\) even: let \(V'\) be the piece of \(B_Q\setminus\{y\}\) away from \(B'\), with \(y\) adjoined; it is even-ended with \(|V'|\ge3\), and \(G:=\evens(V')\) isolates the odd legs of \(V'\). An oo pair from such a leg to \(A'\) is clean by o-cleanness; to \(B'\) it is in \(\sep_{B_Q}(\{y\})\); an opposite-parity pair is in \(\rect{Q}\) or in \(\sep_{B_Q}(\{y\})\). So \(M\cap S_G=\emptyset\): impossible. Case \(\tau\) odd: \(B'\) lies in one run; any other run \(V'\) is even-ended, and if \(|V'|\ge3\), \(G:=\evens(V')\) gives the same contradiction. So \(|V'|=1\). Corollary: the new pairs of \(\rect{Q^+}\) join an even leg of \(B_Q\setminus R_\natural\) to an odd leg of \(R_\natural\) and are in \(\sep_{B_Q}(\tau)\); every missing ee pair inside \(B_Q\) avoids the odd \(\tau\) and lies in one run of at least two legs, i.e. in \(R_\natural\), so \(\ein(Q^+)=\ein(Q)\); and \(\oin(Q)\subseteq\oin(Q^+)\subseteq\oin(P')=\oin(Q)\). ∎

Lemma 8.13 (absorption). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\) and \(P^*\) be innermost. Every o-clean \(Q\in\cP_1\) (with respect to \(P^*\)) with \(A_Q\subseteq A^*\) is in \(K^*\).

Proof. If \(Q\ne P^*\), then \(Q\notin\cS_B\) (innermost), and the Corollary of Lemma 8.12 gives \(Q^+\) with the same missing flank set and a larger odd-ended side inside \(A^*\). Iterate; even-ended sides shrink strictly, so the iteration stops, and only at a chord \(Z\in\cS_B\) with \(A_Z\subseteq A^*\) and \(h_Z=h_Q\). Innermostness forces \(A_Z=A^*\), so \(Z=P^*\) and \(Q\in K^*\). ∎

Lemma 8.14 (lift). Let \(P^*\in\cS_B\), \(P\in K^*\) with \(A_P\subsetneq A^*\), and \(\Theta\) an \(M\)-free even separator of \(A_P\) such that the pairs of \(\oin(P)\) lie in at least two runs. Then \(\Theta^*:=\Theta\cup\evens(A^*\setminus A_P)\) is a spread \(M\)-free even separator of \(A^*\). No hypothesis on \(S\).

Proof. \(A^*\setminus A_P\) consists of one or two even-length arcs from an odd end of \(A^*\) to an even neighbour of \(A_P\); \(\Theta^*\) is a set of at least two even interior legs of \(A^*\). The runs of \(A^*\setminus\Theta^*\) are the odd legs of \(A^*\setminus A_P\) (singletons) and the runs of \(A_P\setminus\Theta\). A pair of \(\sep_{A^*}(\Theta^*)\) with both legs in \(A_P\) is in \(\sep_{A_P}(\Theta)\); otherwise one leg \(u\) is an odd leg of \(A^*\setminus A_P\), and the pair is an oo pair inside \(A^*\) with a leg outside \(A_P\) (clean, since \(\oin(P^*)=\oin(P)\) lies inside \(A_P\)) or a pair in \(\rect{P}\). The occupied runs of \(\Theta\) are occupied runs of \(\Theta^*\). ∎

Proposition 8.15 ((Bot)). Let \(S\in\Fc_N\) with \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\) and \(P^*\) innermost. Then \(K^*\) has a member \(b\) with \(A_b\) linked.

Proof. By Lemma 8.9, (NoSpread) holds. Put \(P_0:=P^*\). Given \(P_k\in K^*\) (so \(A_{P_k}\subseteq A^*\) by Proposition 7.5(a)) with \(A_{P_k}\) not linked, take an \(M\)-free admissible separator \(\Theta\) of \(A_{P_k}\). An odd singleton is excluded by Corollary 8.11. So \(\Theta\) is even, and each pair of \(\oin(P^*)\) lies inside one run. If these pairs lie in two or more runs, (NoSpread) is contradicted directly (\(P_k=P^*\)) or through Lemma 8.14 (\(P_k\ne P^*\)). Otherwise let \(R\) be their one run and \(Q_R\) its run chord: \(\rect{Q_R}\) consists of pairs of \(\rect{P_k}\) and pairs of \(\sep_{A_{P_k}}(\Theta)\), so \(Q_R\in\cP_0\); \(\ein(Q_R)\supseteq\ein(P_k)\ne \emptyset\); \(\oin(Q_R)=\oin(P^*)\); so \(Q_R\in\cP_1\) is o-clean with \(A_{Q_R}\subsetneq A_{P_k}\), and Lemma 8.13 puts it in \(K^*\). Put \(P_{k+1}:=Q_R\). The odd-ended sides shrink strictly, so the process stops at a member \(b\) with \(A_b\) linked. ∎

Conversely (Bot) implies (NoSpread) at an innermost chord; that direction is not used.

9. Minimality

Proposition 9.1 (members of the list). Let \(N\ge6\) be even and \(S_G\in\cG_N\).

  1. \(S_G\) is non-degenerate.
  2. \(\NLSM_N\equiv0\) on \(L_{S_G}\).
  3. \(S_G\) is linearly closed: for every pair \(t\notin S_G\), \(c_t\not\equiv0\) on \(L_{S_G}\).

Proof. (a) Let \(P=(a,b)\) be an odd chord with leg arc \(I=[a,b-1]\), and let \(\mathrm{rep}_I\) be the representation of Proposition 5.6 for the side \(L=I\), read as a coefficient vector on pairs (not the one for the other side; the witnesses below are chosen for \(\mathrm{rep}_I\)). By Corollary 5.2, \(X_P\) vanishes on \(L_{S_G}\) iff \(\mathrm{rep}_I-\kappa\varrho\) is supported on \(S_G\) for some \(\kappa\). This is excluded by exhibiting either (w1) a pair of \(I'\times(I^c)'\) outside \(S_G\), where \(I'\), \((I^c)'\) are the legs of the non-end parity of \(I\), \(I^c\) (opposite-parity pairs, whose coefficient is the same non-zero number in every representation, since \(\varrho\) vanishes on them), or (w2) two pairs \(t_0,t_1\notin S_G\) with \(\mathrm{rep}_I(t_0)=0\ne\varrho(t_0)\), forcing \(\kappa=0\), and \(\mathrm{rep}_I(t_1)\ne0\). For \(G=T\) of one parity: if \(T\) has a leg of the non-end parity inside \(I\), or a leg of the end parity in \(I^c\), a pair through it is a (w1) witness; otherwise \(T\subseteq I\) with the end parity, or \(T\subseteq I^c\) with the other parity; in the first case \(I^c\) lies in one arc of \(T\), and either a (w1) pair inside that arc exists or \(a,b-1\in T\) and \(t_0=(a,j)\) with \(j\in I^c\) of the parity of \(a\), and \(t_1=(a,b-1)\), work; the second case is the mirror. For an odd-chord diamond \(S_{\{u,v\}}\), with \(u\) of the parity of \(a+1\) and \(v\) of the parity of \(a\): if \(u\in I\) or \(v\in I^c\), a pair through \(u\), resp. \(v\), is a (w1) witness; otherwise a (w1) pair inside one arc exists unless \(\{u,v\}=\{a-1,b-1\}\) (the diamond whose corner is \(P\) itself) or \(\{u,v\}=\{b,a\}\) (that diamond shifted by one). In the first case take \(t_0=\{v,j\}\) with \(j\in I^c\) of the parity of \(a\) and not adjacent to \(v\), and \(t_1=\{u,a+1\}\); in the second, \(t_0=\{v,j\}\) as before and \(t_1=\{b,b-2\}\).

(b) For \(S_T\) this is Theorem 2.9. For a causal diamond, the Tr\((\phi^3)\) amplitude vanishes on its locus (the rectangle zero [ACDFH, §3]), and since \(v_0\) lies in the locus, \(X+\delta v_0\) stays in it: every \(\delta\)-coefficient of the numerator vanishes there, while the top coefficient of the denominator is \(\pm\prod_{\mathrm{odd}}X_P\not\equiv0\) by (a); so \(\NLSM_N\equiv0\) there.

(c) \(c_t\equiv0\) on \(L_{S_G}\) iff \(e_t-a=\kappa\varrho\) with \(a\) supported on \(S_G\) (Corollary 5.2); then \(\kappa\ne0\), so every pair outside \(S_G\cup\{t\}\) is opposite-parity and \(t\) is same-parity: \(t\) would be the only same-parity pair outside \(S_G\). But every member has at least two: for \(|T|\ge3\) the pairs within \(T\); for \(|T|=2\) the pair \(T\) and a pair \((t,t+2)\) into the longer arc; for an odd-chord diamond \(\{u,v\}\), the pairs \((u,u+2)\) and \((v,v+2)\). ∎

Lemma 9.2 (antichain). No member of \(\cG_N\) contains another.

Proof. The legs appearing in pairs of \(S_G\) are exactly the legs not in \(G\): every leg of an arc lies on a pair joining two arcs. So \(S_G\subseteq S_{G'}\) gives \(G'\subseteq G\). If \(G'\subsetneq G\), pick \(t\in G\setminus G'\); the pair \(\{t-1,t+1\}\) is in \(S_G\) (its legs lie in different arcs of \(G\)) but not in \(S_{G'}\) (they lie in one arc of \(G'\)). So \(G=G'\). ∎

Proposition 9.3 (clause 2 from clause 1). Let \(N\ge6\) be even and assume clause 1 of Theorem 3.1 at size \(N\). Then every member of \(\cG_N\) is a minimal non-degenerate coordinate zero.

Proof. Let \(S_G\in\cG_N\), \(t_0\in S_G\), and \(S:=S_G\setminus\{t_0\}\); its missing set contains \(t_0\). If some member \(S_{G'}\) had \(S_{G'}\cap M=\emptyset\), then \(S_{G'}\subseteq S\subsetneq S_G\), against Lemma 9.2. So \(M\) meets every member, and \(S\in\Fc_N\) (Proposition 4.4). By clause 1, \(\NLSM_N\not\equiv0\) on \(L_S\). With Proposition 9.1(a), (b), \(S_G\) is a minimal non-degenerate zero. ∎

Proof of Theorem 3.1, clause 2. Clause 1 holds at every even \(N\) (§7.7), so Proposition 9.3 applies. Clause 1 never uses clause 2, so there is no circularity.

Interpretation. In words: every listed zero is tight. Setting one fewer invariant to zero than a causal diamond or a \(T\)-separated locus requires already makes the amplitude non-zero, and no listed locus contains another, so none of the zeros is a consequence of the others by restriction.

10. Discussion and outlook

10.1 Three questions a referee will ask

Is clause 2 unconditional? Clause 2 needs two vanishing statements. For causal diamonds it uses the Tr\((\phi^3)\) diamond zero [ACDFH, §3], which has several published derivations (§1) and is also proved in the project's Lean library for every \(n\). For the \(T\)-separated sets it uses Theorem 2.9. For \(|T|=3\) this is [CEU] (their CHY argument does not discuss degenerate solutions of the scattering equations), for \(T\) a whole parity class it is stated in [CHZ, §IV.1], and for general \(T\) it rests on the companion note, whose proof is reproduced in Supplement S2 and has been reviewed once, blind, by another model instance of the same family. Clause 1 depends on the same theorem, applied to sub-polygons in the ear lemma and in the value of the strip factor (Lemma 7.14, Theorem 7.17). So the written proof is conditional on exactly one statement outside this paper, and that statement is proved in the supplement. The Lean proof of Theorem 3.1 is not conditional on it: it proves Theorem 2.9 and the diamond zero itself, for every even \(N\) and every \(T\) (Methods). The written proof of Theorem 2.9 is still the largest part of the written argument that no reader outside the project has checked.

Why coordinate loci, and why non-degenerate ones? Coordinate loci are the loci of the hidden-zero programme: conditions \(s_{ab}=0\) on invariants of non-adjacent legs, the tiles of the mesh. For them the question “which are zeros” has a finite answer at each \(N\), and the theorem gives it. Non-degeneracy is forced by the question: on a locus where some odd planar variable vanishes identically, the amplitude has a propagator that is identically infinite there, the restriction is not defined, and “vanishes on the locus” has no meaning. Zeros on linear loci that are not coordinate loci do exist for Tr\((\phi^3)\): on a 2-split locus of [CDHS] the amplitude factorises into lower-point currents, and a zero of one factor is a zero of the whole amplitude on a linear locus that is in general not a coordinate locus; computations within the project found such zeros at five points (on lines), at six (by iterating the split of [ACDFH]) and at seven (nested in a 2-split). We have not studied the analogous question for the NLSM systematically (a small check at eight points found some of these nested loci to be NLSM zeros and others not; Supplement S4), and the theorem says nothing about it: “complete” means complete among coordinate loci.

What is the physics of the class residue, and is it a split? In a smooth split [CEU] or a 2-split [CDHS] the amplitude, restricted to a locus defined by orthogonality conditions, factorises into lower-point currents without taking a residue. The class residue of Theorem 7.9 is a different object: it is the residue of the restricted amplitude on a hyperplane where a whole nested family of channels goes on shell at once. We read the two as sharing a mechanism. The strip legs between two channels of the class are orthogonal to one side or the other by the conditions that \(S\) imposes (Lemma 7.12), which is split-like kinematics for the strip regions, and it is this that makes the strips collapse to \((-1)^d\) and drop out of the residue. We have not tried to derive the class residue from a split, or conversely; the resemblance suggests that the residue may be a limit of a smooth split, and that question is open. The value \((-1)^d\) in the \(\NLSM\) normalisation, which becomes \(+1\) in the normalisation \(\cA_N\), is the key ingredient: it is what makes the residue exactly a product of two amplitudes of the same theory.

10.2 Outlook

  1. One regime instead of two. For a candidate set with \(\cP_1\ne\emptyset\) in which every missing same-parity pair lies inside a side of a \(\cP_1\) chord of the matching type, is the first-order term \(G_S\) of Theorem 6.8(a) non-zero? It would give a second proof of clause 1 in which the case \(\cP_1=\emptyset\) is a special case. It holds on every case computed (Supplement S4) and is open in general; the residue route does not prove it, since the residue factors of the first-order term are not \(\pm\) the children's first-order terms.
  2. EFT corrections and the double copy. Zero conditions constrain higher-derivative corrections to the NLSM, and the zeros extend via the KLT relations to the special Galileon [BBKOPT]. Whether the special Galileon vanishes on the \(T\)-separated loci with \(|T|\ge3\) is not proved here and is open; computations in the companion note, in a part not reproduced in the supplement, suggest a zero there only in a restricted, direction-dependent sense. Whether its coordinate zeros, or those of gravity-type or other exceptional theories, admit a complete list like \(\cG_N\) has not been studied.
  3. Loop level. Completeness is a tree-level statement; a loop analogue has not been formulated.
  4. The Tr\((\phi^3)\) analogue. That every non-degenerate coordinate zero of Tr\((\phi^3)\) contains a causal diamond is proved within the project only in part: by exhaustive computation for \(n\le10\), and for every \(n\) when \(S\) avoids a vertex. So the question is open for the simpler theory; the pion proof relies throughout on the parity structure of the \(\delta\)-shift, which Tr\((\phi^3)\) lacks.
  5. A shorter descent. Can the shortening step (Lemma 8.8) be removed from Lemma 8.9 by a choice of the runs \(\RX,\RY\) (for example adjacent occupied runs) or of the chord \(P^*\)? Open and untested.
  6. Formalisation. Theorem 3.1 is machine-checked (Methods and provenance). What remains is for a person to check that the Lean definitions of the amplitude, the coordinate loci, non-degeneracy, zeros and \(\cG_N\) say what §2 says; lean/README.md lists where they are.

11. Relation to prior work

Every statement below concerns a source that was opened in the project's cache and is quoted with its location.

  • Tr\((\phi^3)\), small \(n\). [ACDFH, §3.1.2] summarise the zeros found at five and six points: “We can see that all the zeros patterns are causal diamonds that extend to the boundaries of the mesh picture.” For the NLSM, [ACDFH, §4.1] carry the diamonds over. Neither passage claims a complete list for the NLSM, and the NLSM has coordinate zeros that contain no diamond (the \(S_T\) with \(|T|\ge3\)).
  • Three arcs. [CEU, §4.1, §4.3–§4.4] show the \(|T|=3\) zero from the CHY formula; their argument works with the separated CHY integrals and does not discuss degenerate solutions of the scattering equations. For the biadjoint amplitude they ask, in §6.2, “can one give a complete enumeration and interpretation of all kinematic subspaces \(\mathcal K'\) such that the restriction \(m_n(\mathbb I,\mathbb I)|_{\mathcal K'}\) is proportional to the product of a cyclic chain of currents” — a completeness question about factorisation loci of another amplitude, not about NLSM zeros.
  • Splits. [CDHS, §2] derive the diamond zeros of Tr\((\phi^3)\), NLSM and YMS from the 2-split: “the zeros of color-ordered amplitudes of Tr \(\phi^3\), NLSM or YMS theory correspond to \(s_{a,b}=0\) for \(i\lt a\lt j\) and \(j\lt b\le n\) … which is precisely given by a ‘rectangle’ in the mesh picture”. No completeness statement.
  • Zeros and UV scaling; uniqueness. [Rod, §II]: “There are different types of zeros, classified by the number of invariants involved”; App. A proves “that all zeros are equivalent to enhanced scaling under some corresponding BCFW shift”. From §II, “all zeros” there means the zeros of [ACDFH]; it is not a classification. The same paper, Li and Rodina for cosmological wavefunctions (“locality together with the full set of hidden zeros uniquely fixes tree-level cosmological wavefunctions without assuming unitarity” [LR, abstract]), and Li and Zhou for the NLSM (“the hidden zeros, combined with standard factorization on physical poles, uniquely determine all tree-level NLSM amplitudes” [LZ, abstract]) run in the converse direction: they fix a function from a given set of zeros. Theorem 3.1 is a statement about the zero set of one fixed function. The two directions are complementary: a complete list is the input such a bootstrap can use.
  • “All zeros” of string amplitudes. Chang, Chen and Huang [CCH] study the zeros of string amplitudes through their curve-integral representation: higher-level amplitudes share “the same set of zeros as the \(n\)-point tachyon amplitude as well as those inherited from it's prescaffold image” (abstract), and “the super-tachyon enjoys the same set of zeros as the bosonic tachyon amplitude, with additional zeros from the pffafian structure” (§4.1). “All zeros” there means the zeros inherited through scaffolding; the paper does not show that no other zeros exist, and it makes no statement about the NLSM.
  • Bootstrap with zeros and splits. Berman, Elvang and Figueiredo [BEF] impose the hidden zero and the 5-point splitting conditions in a numerical S-matrix bootstrap of \(2\to2\) EFT scattering and find that “the string beta function is the unique 4-point amplitude compatible with hidden zero and the 5-point splitting constraints” in the absence of single-mass infinite spin towers (abstract). This uses zeros as constraints; it does not classify the zeros of a given amplitude.
  • Not all zeros are hidden zeros. [Zho26, §2]: “The hidden zeros for a given amplitude do not exhaust all zeros but instead represent a proper subset thereof”, for YM and NLSM, with no example. For non-degenerate coordinate zeros of the NLSM, the \(S_T\) with \(|T|\ge3\) are examples, and Theorem 3.1 says that together with the diamonds they are all the minimal ones.
  • An open question. [FZZ, §4]: “One can also ask are there other zero conditions different from the one found in current literature?” Theorem 3.1 bears on this question for the non-degenerate coordinate zeros of the tree-level NLSM; it does not address other loci or other theories.
  • The members of the list. \(|T|=2\) and the odd-chord diamonds: [ACDFH, §4.1]; \(|T|=3\): [CEU]; \(T\) a whole parity class: stated in [CHZ, §IV.1]; general \(T\): the companion note (Supplement S2).

Novelty. A completeness statement for the coordinate zeros of the NLSM, or of Tr\((\phi^3)\), was not found in the sources above. A search aimed at such statements (arXiv and INSPIRE metadata and abstracts, and a text search of the project's cache of about 140 papers, in September 2026) found none either; papers that were not cached were judged by their abstracts only. That is all we claim; we do not claim that the result is new.

  • Authors. Every statement, proof and paragraph was written by instances of AI models, not by a person. The model of record is claude-opus-5-5 (Anthropic), except for two items written by claude-fable-5-1 (Anthropic): a second proof of the reach lemma (Lemma 8.3) and the classification of proportional odd-chord pairs (the remark after Lemma 7.6, not used in the proof).
